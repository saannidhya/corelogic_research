The many meanings of cash: literature and contribution review

Review date: November 2, 2026. Inputs: the eight-page June 6, 2026 manuscript PDF; project research specification and June positioning memo; and the recent research ideation, supplied by the author. This is a broad, targeted literature review and research assessment, not a systematic review or a replication audit. The accompanying reading list identifies actual reading coverage and versions for 46 papers.

Assessment

The project has a useful empirical starting point: a national transaction taxonomy linked to subsequent property transactions. Its most promising contribution is to explain how mortgage-market frictions change the route by which a home reaches its next long-term owner. That would connect household finance to housing intermediation and market liquidity. The present tables motivate that question; they do not yet establish the mechanism.

Three proposed contributions are already substantially occupied: cash purchases are heterogeneous; cash discounts reflect financing frictions and selection; and some investors buy cheaply, improve or intermediate homes, and resell. The opportunity is to identify the connection between these mechanisms: when direct mortgage-financed trade becomes harder, does a cash intermediary enable a transaction that otherwise would not occur, delay access by mortgaged households, or divert the property into rental ownership? These possibilities have different implications for prices, turnover, and access to homeownership.

I would retain the taxonomy as the measurement foundation, elevate the property transition panel, and select a financing-policy experiment only after checking its first stage and assignment rule. I would not organize the next paper around the claim that buyer composition resolves the mortgage–cash premium puzzle. Nor would I implement the proposed conforming-limit sale-price RD without redesigning it.

The closest missing papers are Seo–Holmes–Lee; Jauregui–Tidwell–Sah; Tidwell–Jauregui–Sah–Narwold; Ambrose–Chia; Bayer–Geissler–Mangum–Roberts; and Buchak–Matvos–Piskorski–Seru. They respectively constrain the novelty of the taxonomy, financing decomposition, distress adjustment, credit-access mechanism, flipper interpretation, and intermediary story. Recent work by Zhu, Moktan–Guin–Clarke, and Díaz further narrows the appraisal, disaster, and credit/inventory angles. Links and the substantive comparisons follow.

1. What the current manuscript establishes

The paper combines a large national CoreLogic transaction base with within-cell price comparisons and repeat-transaction outcomes. Its categories prioritize distressed cash, then investor cash, corporate cash, and ordinary cash, with mortgage purchases as the benchmark. This is a mutually exclusive reporting partition constructed from overlapping economic dimensions: financing, legal form, investor activity, and distress. A corporation is not necessarily a large institution; an individual can be an investor. The category names should not silently become claims about economic functions.

The current evidence has two distinct levels:

  • Price composition: Tables 2–3 show large differences across cash categories and changes in the aggregate cash–mortgage gap after exclusions. These are descriptive price gaps within the paper's comparison cells. They are not estimates of the causal effect of paying cash.
  • Subsequent transactions: Table 4 shows shorter observed holding periods and larger gross resale appreciation for several cash groups than for mortgage purchasers. Many observed resales are mortgage-financed. This supports investigating intermediation, but does not identify its contribution to market liquidity or welfare.

Several features matter for positioning. The exclusion sequence in Table 3 changes support and weighting; it is not an additive decomposition of the aggregate gap. The displayed weighted gap does not even move monotonically at every exclusion. Build a common-support, fixed-weight accounting exercise before assigning shares of the gap to mechanisms. Report separately the effect of changing group shares, within-group gaps, and sample support. Even that would be an accounting decomposition, not a causal one.

The resale panel conditions on a subsequent observed sale within a bounded holding window. It therefore underrepresents long-term rental holdings and unresolved inventory, while the six-month lower bound excludes the fastest flips. Later cohorts have less follow-up; partial 2024 coverage needs explicit treatment. Use all eligible acquisitions as the denominator, with equal follow-up windows or a documented censoring strategy.

The paper's HPI-adjusted gains are gross capital appreciation, not net profits or intermediation rents. State-level appreciation adjustments cannot remove renovation expenditure, acquisition discounts, neighborhood shocks, taxes, fees, carrying costs, or selected resale timing. A subsequent purchaser's mortgage differs from the same owner's delayed financing. These events need separate labels. Mortgage financing also does not establish owner occupancy.

Finally, Table 4's mortgage-buyer resale benchmark has a higher mortgage-financed resale share than the cash categories. The observation that many investor resales use mortgages is therefore not, by itself, evidence of an unusually strong bridge into mortgage finance. The relevant question is the counterfactual probability and timing of a mortgaged household obtaining that home.

These are interpretations of the supplied PDF, not newly computed results. Historical availability of classification and property fields, entity linkage, and look-ahead in ownership flags remain data questions to audit.

2. The closest literature and what it leaves open

Cash prices, financing risk, and selection

Reher and Valkanov (2024), Journal of Finance. Their central object is a mortgage premium associated with transaction frictions, assessed through multiple datasets/designs, a seller model, and survey evidence. Published Table IV already excludes institutional transactions and flips in separate checks, and includes demanding property/location-time controls. Their evidence cannot be dismissed as an unfiltered national cash average. The project should reproduce comparable restrictions and estimands before suggesting that composition explains their finding. The potential distinction is a dynamic property-allocation outcome rather than another average price coefficient. Coverage: published local PDF selectively read, especially introduction and Table IV. Paper.

Han and Hong (2024), Review of Finance. Their main Los Angeles analysis focuses on individual buyers and excludes foreclosures. They connect a cash discount and shorter agreement-to-closing time to financing risk interacting with the possibility of failing to sell after a transaction collapses. This makes closing risk and housing search inseparable. Time to close is not time on market, and neither equals an investor's holding period. National coverage alone is a weak distinction because they also examine external validity beyond Los Angeles. A contribution would measure subsequent ownership allocation or a separately identified loanability channel. Coverage: published local PDF selectively read, including sample restrictions, Tables 2/6/7 and section 5.5. Paper.

Seo, Holmes, and Lee (2023; online 2021), Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics. This is a direct predecessor to “many meanings.” Their Tallahassee 2006–2015 analysis uses propensity-score matching and distinguishes price segments, distressed properties, investor purchases, and rentals. Discounts concentrate in lower-priced housing rather than appearing uniformly across categories. A larger taxonomy alone is not a new economic insight. The remaining opportunity is national comparability, better measurement, dynamic transitions, and credible policy variation. Matching addresses observed selection, not every reason buyers choose financing. Coverage: full text selectively read via PMC. Published record; open text.

Jauregui, Tidwell, and Hite (2017); Tidwell, Jauregui, Sah, and Narwold (2018); Jauregui, Tidwell, and Sah (2019). These are essential methodological predecessors. They examine selection in cash transactions, joint cash/distress selection and spatial dependence, and differences among cash, conventional, FHA, and VA financing. The 2019 paper already uses a Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition. Therefore “we control for distress,” “selection matters,” or “we decompose financing groups” cannot carry the contribution. A decomposition in this project must clarify its estimand and improve measurement or answer a new allocation question. Their selection-model assumptions should be evaluated rather than adopted as automatic identification. Coverage: publisher abstracts and metadata, not full-text methods audits. 2017, 2018, 2019.

Ambrose and Chia (2025 revision), working paper. Their paper connects larger discounts in low-priced housing to small-mortgage availability and uses post-crisis mortgage-fraud enforcement variation as an instrument for local small-credit supply. This is the closest competitor to a generic “credit constraints explain cash discounts” contribution. A different policy instrument alone would offer limited differentiation. A stronger distinction is whether credit access changes direct sales versus intermediary acquisition and eventual household ownership. The proposed instrument's exclusion restriction needs full-text scrutiny; enforcement could affect other local housing conditions. Coverage: SSRN abstract and author listing; full text not obtained. Current record.

Earlier foundations. Asabere–Huffman–Mehdiany (1992), Lusht–Hansz (1994), Bian–Lin–Liu (2018), and Aroul–Hansz (2023) establish that cash pricing, mortgage contingencies, bargaining, and cyclicality predate the two 2024 finance papers. Include them in the intellectual history without treating their estimates as directly comparable across samples. 1992, 1994, 2018, 2023.

Intermediaries, investors, and distressed assets

Bayer, Geissler, Mangum, and Roberts (2020), Review of Financial Studies. Their distinction between middlemen and speculators is central: experienced intermediaries' acquisition/resale strategies and improvements differ from speculative market timing. Short holdings plus large appreciation already have an extensive interpretation in this literature. The project's advance would be to identify when financing frictions cause intermediation, and what happens to properties that do not promptly resell. Coverage: published abstract; selective reading of an older NBER version, not the final published methods. Paper.

Buchak, Matvos, Piskorski, and Seru (2026), Journal of Political Economy. Cite the published October 2026 article, not solely the earlier NBER paper. The iBuyer setting directly studies housing intermediation and its limits, including the tension between rapid execution and information/selection. The distinction available here is broader financing-contingent intermediation outside identifiable iBuyers. A generic fast-cash intermediary mechanism is already occupied. Coverage: final publication metadata verified; older NBER revision selectively skimmed. Published paper; working-paper lineage.

Leung and Tse (2017) model flipping, and Díaz (2026) links credit, forward-looking intermediary inventories, and trading speed. If a model is added, its new ingredient must be explicit—such as property loanability and conversion into mortgage-eligible household demand—rather than simply the existence of intermediaries. Coverage: abstracts/metadata. Flipping; credit and inventories.

Campbell, Giglio, and Pathak (2011) is the forced-sale benchmark. Counterweights are Harding, Rosenblatt, and Yao (2012), whose analysis questions economically meaningful excess returns from most REO purchases, and Conklin, Coulson, Diop, and Mota (2023), which uses appraisal information to address property quality in distress discounts. These papers make renovation, condition, and net costs first-order issues. They do not mechanically refute the manuscript's larger gaps, because samples and estimands differ. They do make “large observed discount equals liquidity rent” untenable. Forced sales, REO returns, quality and distress.

Lambie-Hanson, Li, and Slonkosky (2022) studies investor participation in the housing recovery. Mills, Molloy, and Zarutskie (2019) provides the large buy-to-rent benchmark. Garriga, Gete, and Tsouderou (2023), Lee and Wylie (2024), and Gorback, Qian, and Zhu (2025 revision) connect investor ownership to broader housing-market outcomes. This literature requires separating intermediary inventory from durable rental ownership. An LLC flag cannot do that. The project's possible contribution is the financing-induced transition between these roles, not a general assertion that investors affect housing markets. Recovery, buy-to-rent, economic effects, neighborhoods, institutional owners.

Search, seller constraints, and information

The conceptual backbone is Wheaton (1990), Stein (1995), Krainer (2001), Genesove–Mayer (1997, 2001), and Han–Strange (2015): matching frictions, down-payment constraints, seller equity, loss aversion, and bargaining jointly determine prices and trading. These mechanisms matter especially for the 2022-rate idea because existing owners' decisions change alongside prospective buyers' financing. Jiang–Kotova–Zhang (2024) warns against inferring liquidity from a single price or duration measure. Mateen–Qian–Zhang–Zheng (2026 revision) supplies offer-level evidence, reminding us that completed transactions omit rejected offers and failed deals. Wheaton, Stein, Krainer, equity, loss aversion, survey, liquidity measures, offers.

Kurlat–Stroebel (2015), Chinco–Mayer (2016), and Giacoletti (2021) provide alternative interpretations involving information, speculative mistakes, and idiosyncratic risk. Differences between repeat local buyers and distant or inexperienced purchasers need not be liquidity services. These belong in the mechanism discussion and the interpretation of resale returns. Information, speculation, risk.

Recent competitors worth monitoring

Zhu (January 2026) studies spillovers from cash comparable sales to appraisals and subsequent mortgage transactions. An appraisal-spillover contribution would therefore need a distinct intervention or stronger identification. Aldana–Zhu (2025) studies cash purchases' effects on mortgage lenders; lender outcomes can be endogenous to cash activity. Loschi (October 29, 2026, v3.12) examines related-party transfers and the NYC cash–mortgage gap. It is a very recent self-archived working paper with limited financing-validation evidence, not an established benchmark; nonetheless, it directly motivates transparent family-transfer and entity-transfer exclusions. Appraisals, lenders, NYC measurement.

RQ1. Fonseca and Liu's mortgage lock-in evidence makes the seller side unavoidable. Higher rates change both prospective purchasers' financing costs and incumbent owners' incentives to list or move. Anenberg and Ringo emphasize transmission through connected housing demand. An increase in cash share therefore cannot distinguish cash entry from a disappearance of mortgaged trades. Report cash and mortgage transaction counts per pre-period housing stock, total turnover, and buyer composition alongside shares. Geography and time effects, matched pretrends, and placebo dates are useful diagnostics; they do not make pre-existing exposure random. Lock-in; demand propagation.

RQ2. The ideation's threshold conforming loan limit / 0.80 assumes a common down payment. Buyers can adjust down payments and loan structure; transaction price is negotiated and potentially affected by treatment. Cash transactions have no observed loan assignment. A density test around that price cannot validate the missing assignment mechanism. DeFusco and Paciorek explicitly study endogenous bunching in loan amounts; Adelino, Schoar, and Severino exploit mortgage-market segmentation. These are design references, not validation of the proposed price RD. Start with historical policy changes, actual mortgage terms, and values measured before the change. Show that the proposed exposure predicts financing access, check geographic comparability and spillovers, and describe the result as an exposure-based policy design unless an actual discontinuity in treatment assignment is demonstrated. Bunching; segmentation, published 2025.

RQ3. Moktan, Guin, and Clarke already examine post-Grenfell lending contraction and cash substitution. Their paper itself distinguishes the overall shock from a separately identified credit-supply effect. Ortega–Taşpınar and Hino–Burke show why physical damage and risk information cannot be swept into mortgage access. A viable design would need, for example, a well-documented eligibility/remediation rule plus comparable physical damage and insurance exposure. Public disaster polygons alone provide event exposure, not an excluded financing shock. Lending and cash, Sandy, risk information.

RQ4. A corporate-cash indicator cannot identify an iBuyer. The design needs historical entity aliases, entry/exit dates, acquisition exposure set before exit, and treatment of inventory liquidation. A national corporate exit can still create locally endogenous treatment intensity. Establish the institutional event before relying on staggered treatment methods. Existing iBuyer work makes net service provision, inventory risk, and substitution toward other intermediaries more interesting outcomes than a cash discount.

RQ5. A concrete candidate for screening is the 2015 FHA insurance-premium reduction examined by Bhutta and Ringo, rather than an unspecified lender overlay. Fuster and Zafar also distinguish financing constraints affecting housing demand. The innovation would be downstream property allocation, not rediscovering mortgage-demand sensitivity. The current post-2018 comparison sample would need extension, consistent historical fields, and adequate post-policy follow-up. Define eligibility or exposure before policy; do not define treatment using borrowers' realized choice to use FHA afterward. Distinguish cheaper insurance from improved access and from changes in property eligibility. This is a feasibility recommendation, not an assertion that the policy provides a valid instrument for cash intermediation in these data. FHA policy; financing conditions.

4. A contribution that could survive the closest comparisons

Preferred research question: When mortgage access changes, do homes trade directly to households, pass through cash intermediaries, or remain in rental/inventory ownership—and what happens to the timing and cost of eventual household acquisition?

The causal object should initially be the effect of an identified financing-policy change on these property outcomes. It should not automatically be the effect of “being bought with cash”: financing policy can directly affect sellers, buyers, prices, and rental demand, so using it as an instrument for cash acquisition requires additional exclusion restrictions. Estimate reduced-form policy effects first. Distinguishing mediation through cash intermediaries requires further assumptions or variation.

Three observable patterns would help distinguish mechanisms:

  1. Bridge/intermediation: the shock increases intermediary acquisitions and subsequent transfers to mortgaged households within fixed horizons, with evidence on turnover that rules out merely relabeling transactions that would otherwise have occurred directly.
  2. Rental diversion/displacement: investor acquisitions increase, but long-term holding or rental use rises and household acquisition falls. A rise in cash share alone cannot distinguish this from a bridge.
  3. Ordinary household financing substitution: cash acquisitions by validated non-investor households rise without intermediary resale or repair. Separate later same-owner liens from resale to another purchaser.

These are proposed discriminating tests, not findings. In particular, faster observed resale conditional on resale supports none of them by itself. Measuring welfare would additionally require counterfactual matching, costs, rents/housing services, and who gains or loses. The current dataset should not be promised to deliver welfare without those components.

5. Concrete next work and decision gates

First, establish measurement before running more price regressions. Construct a validation sample across states, years, price levels, and buyer types. Check cash flags against recorded purchase-money liens and timing; quantify unknown financing. Absence of a recorded purchase mortgage does not establish an unlevered buyer: credit lines, portfolio borrowing, or other collateral can fund an acquisition. Nor does the recorded financing outcome establish whether the original offer contained a mortgage contingency. Separate delayed financing, non-arm's-length transfers, nominal consideration, bulk transactions, and ambiguous entities. Retain overlapping investor/corporate/distress indicators alongside the reporting hierarchy. Check that ownership and property characteristics are measured as of acquisition rather than taken from later snapshots. Report uncertain classifications instead of forcing every observation into an economic type.

Second, reconcile the pricing evidence. Produce one table holding geography-time support, weights, price transformation, property controls, and transfer filters fixed. Then apply restrictions comparable to the closest cash papers. Display both the full national descriptive gap and the standardized comparison gap. If a large discrepancy survives, identify the data or estimand responsible before declaring a new puzzle. A price-level difference, median percentage gap, and log-price coefficient should not share the same label without conversion and denominator definitions.

Third, build the transition panel on all acquisitions. At fixed 12-, 24-, and 36-month horizons, classify no observed transfer, resale to a mortgage buyer, resale to another cash buyer, and additional repeat transfers; separately record same-owner refinancing. Add validated occupancy/rental indicators where available. Make missing follow-up and unresolved ownership explicit. Start with cohorts whose horizon is fully observed and investigate faster-than-six-month resales rather than excluding them by construction. Treat prices/gains among resellers as selected outcomes alongside unconditional transition probabilities.

Fourth, screen two financing designs rather than commit to the easiest regression. For an FHA policy and a conforming-eligibility policy, document the rule, affected borrowers/properties, dates, predetermined assignment/exposure, financing first stage, competing policies, and comparison group. Use a small feasibility extract through the authorized loader. The go/no-go condition is a defensible assignment mechanism plus measurable downstream transitions, not statistical significance in cash share. A national 2022 event study remains useful context if neither policy design survives.

Fifth, add mechanism data selectively. Prioritize mortgage linkage and historical entity/occupancy classification for the preferred question. Permits/condition data become essential if the claim is rehabilitation; MLS agreement/listing/failure data become essential if the claim is certainty and search. Avoid promising all mechanisms with the same deed-only proxy.

If these gates succeed, the paper structure should be: economic question and competing mechanisms; measurement and benchmark reconciliation; financing intervention and first stage; unconditional property transitions; prices/costs and mechanism tests; distributional implications and limits. If causal identification fails, a carefully validated national measurement paper remains possible, but it should present itself as descriptive and demonstrate why its distinctions change an economically important conclusion.

Then read Bayer et al. and Buchak et al. before interpreting Table 4.
