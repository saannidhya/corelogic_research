\begin{abstract}
\noindent Between 2014 and 2021, iBuyers became the first intermediaries to quote firm, capital-backed prices generated by statistical models at scale in the largest decentralized asset market. I ask whether the machine's quotes sharpened price discovery for households who never traded with it. A model of algorithmic intermediation with adverse selection predicts that the machine sorts into homogeneous segments, that forecast-error variance taxes its spread (so the highest-error algorithm exits first when home prices become unpredictable), and that its public purchase prices act as signals that compress price dispersion among third-party trades---by an amount that scales with the machine's tiny share of comparable sales. I take the model to 42 million deed records across 22 states, using the abrupt November 2021 shutdown of Zillow Offers, with ZIPs exposed to the surviving machines as controls. The cleanly identified result is a precise null: when the machine's quotes vanished, within-ZIP price dispersion did not measurably rise. The effect is statistically zero with a clean pre-period placebo, and the bound is economically tight---at the machine's reach of under two percent of comparable-sale windows, the data cap the externality below one percent of baseline dispersion, sharp enough to reject the model's most aggressive calibrations and to confirm its central message: an algorithmic dealer at this scale is too small a part of the comparable-sale record to move price discovery. Household transaction volume also fell sharply in exposed ZIPs, but exposed markets were on a differential downward path before the shutdown, so I bound rather than assert a causal liquidity effect. The lesson is not that the machine's information was worthless but that, at the scale the industry reached, what the market measurably lost when the algorithm died was a balance sheet, not a signal---and the advisory Zestimate, the signal, never went offline. \\
\textbf{JEL classification:} D47, D82, D83, G14, L85, R31 \\
\textbf{Keywords:} algorithmic intermediation, iBuyers, price discovery, housing search, adverse selection, AVM
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The United States housing stock is worth roughly \$50 trillion, and the market that prices it has no market maker. Every house is different. The typical owner trades once a decade. The information that determines value---the slope of the lot, the smell in the basement, the neighbor's plans---is dispersed across millions of households and revealed only through bilateral search and bargaining. Prices in this market aggregate information slowly and badly: house price changes are forecastable \citep{case1989efficiency}, momentum persists for years \citep{guren2018momentum, glaeser2017extrapolative}, sellers learn about demand only by listing and waiting \citep{anenberg2016information}, and the idiosyncratic component of individual house returns is enormous \citep{giacoletti2021idiosyncratic}. The microstructure of this market---search, bargaining, brokerage---has been studied for decades \citep{wheaton1990vacancy, krainer2001theory, genesove2012search, han2015microstructure}. What it never had was an agent willing to post a firm price.

Between 2014 and 2021 it briefly got one. iBuyers---Opendoor, Offerpad, Zillow Offers, RedfinNow---deployed automated valuation models (AVMs) that generated instant, binding cash offers on owner-occupied homes, backed by the firms' own balance sheets. A homeowner entered an address, received a price within days, and could close in a week. This is not a Zestimate. An advisory estimate is a newsletter; an iBuyer quote is a dealer's bid. The firm commits capital against its forecast, takes the house into inventory, and resells it. At peak, the machines were large: iBuyers bought roughly 70{,}000 homes in 2021, about 1.3 percent of all U.S. home purchases, and in Phoenix---the industry's birthplace and deepest market---12.1 percent of homes sold in the third quarter of 2021 moved through an iBuyer. For the first time in the history of the largest consumer asset market, a statistical model quoted executable prices at scale.

This paper asks what the machine's presence did to the market around it. The question is the one \citet{hendershott2011algorithmic} asked of algorithmic trading in equities---does the algorithm improve liquidity and make quotes more informative?---transplanted to a decentralized search market where the answer need not be the same. In a centralized limit-order book, algorithmic liquidity provision narrows spreads and speeds price discovery \citep{hendershott2011algorithmic, brogaard2014hft}, though fast trading can also impose adverse-selection externalities \citep{biais2015fast}. Housing is the opposite environment: no consolidated tape, no order book, heterogeneous assets, and counterparties who know things about their own house that no model can see \citep{kurlat2015testing, stroebel2016asymmetric}. Whether a machine that quotes firm prices against better-informed homeowners sharpens or distorts price discovery for third parties is a genuinely open question. Theory cuts both ways: a dealer's quote impounds information into public view \citep{glosten1985bid, kyle1985continuous}, but an algorithm that free-rides on past transaction prices inherits the \citet{grossman1980impossibility} limit, and \citet{akerlof1970lemons} selection bounds what it can touch.

The question has a second half that makes it answerable. On November 2, 2021, Zillow announced that it was shutting down Zillow Offers---then the second-largest iBuyer, operating in 25 metropolitan areas---after a quarter in which it bought 9{,}680 homes, sold 3{,}032, and wrote down its inventory by \$304 million. CEO Rich Barton's explanation was a statement about forecasting, not about housing demand: ``We've determined the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated and continuing to scale Zillow Offers would result in too much earnings and balance-sheet volatility.'' The shutdown was national, abrupt, and firm-specific. Opendoor and Offerpad kept buying in the same ZIP codes. And critically, Zillow's advisory signal---the Zestimate, displayed on every listing in America---stayed online. The exit removed the firm, capital-backed quote while leaving the free advisory estimate in place. It is the cleanest separation we are likely to get between the two channels through which an algorithm can inform a market, and to my knowledge no paper has used it.

I make three contributions. The first is a theory of algorithmic intermediation in a decentralized market. A machine runs an AVM with bounded forecast error and posts a firm bid, net of a spread, to sellers who privately observe their house's idiosyncratic value. Sellers differ in urgency; the machine resells at fundamental value. The model delivers four results. First, the machine optimally prices so that only urgent sellers---movers---accept, and its profit falls with segment heterogeneity: with any fixed cost of operating a market segment, the machine sorts into homogeneous, machine-readable segments. Second, forecast-error variance is a pure tax on the spread, and the machine faces an operational winner's curse---it buys the most precisely when it overprices---so a regime shift that makes prices harder to forecast removes the highest-error algorithm first while precise competitors stay. That is the 2021 collapse in one inequality. Third, because the machine's purchase prices are public deed records, de-spreading them yields a common-value signal for everyone else: machine presence compresses the dispersion of household-to-household prices around fundamentals, and exit raises dispersion in proportion to the machine's pre-exit share. Fourth, intermediation mechanically adds recorded volume (each intermediated house generates two deeds) and absorbs urgent sellers who would otherwise transact at fire-sale discounts, thinning the left tail of the price distribution; exit reverses both.

The second contribution is the deed evidence on entry. I build a ZIP-by-quarter panel from CoreLogic deed records covering 22 iBuyer-active states from 2010 through mid-2024, identify 376{,}256 iBuyer-linked deeds by matching buyer and seller names against entity aliases verified in SEC subsidiary filings, and trace the staggered entry of iBuyers across metropolitan areas with interaction-weighted event studies \citep{sun2021estimating}. Entry is not random---iBuyers chose flat, homogeneous, liquid Sunbelt markets, exactly as the model's sorting result predicts and as prior work documents \citep{seiler2023burgeoning, harrison2024impact}---and the deed panel says so bluntly: joint pre-trend tests reject for most outcomes, so I read the entry event studies as descriptive and place no causal weight on them. The one outcome whose pre-trends are clean (the robust, IQR-based dispersion measure, joint $p = 0.77$) falls by 0.4 log points after entry ($p = 0.08$), the model-consistent sign, with the same sign in all five robustness variants---though as one outcome surviving of five tested, I treat even this as suggestive rather than confirmatory. The entry design's visible selection is itself a finding---it is Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting} in the raw data---and it is why the paper's identification leans entirely on the exit.

The third contribution is the exit experiment. I measure each ZIP code's exposure to the shutdown as Zillow's share of transactions over 2018--2021 and compare high-exposure ZIPs to ZIPs with comparable Opendoor and Offerpad exposure, within metro-by-quarter cells. The Opendoor control absorbs whatever made a ZIP attractive to algorithmic buyers in the first place and the 2022 rate shock that hit iBuyer-heavy Sunbelt markets, because Opendoor-exposed ZIPs took that shock too---their machine just kept operating. The identifying assumption is that, conditional on metro-quarter fixed effects and overall iBuyer attractiveness, the incidence of Zillow's forecasting-driven collapse was uncorrelated with ZIP-level counterfactual trends. The price-discovery outcomes meet that bar: within-ZIP dispersion of household prices rose by a statistically indistinguishable $0.84$ log points per percentage point of Zillow exposure (s.e.\ $1.06$), the composition-free IQR measure moved $-0.20$ (s.e.\ $0.63$), and---crucially---neither shows a differential pre-trend, so the null is identified, not an artifact. The bound is economically tight: the data cap the information externality below about $0.6$ percent of baseline dispersion, sharp enough to reject the model's most aggressive calibrations, while the model fed Zillow's own forecast-error dispersion predicts an effect comfortably inside it. The reading is not that the machine's quotes were uninformative in principle but that, reaching under two percent of comparable-sale windows, they were too small a part of the record to move segment-level price discovery---and their removal did not. Household transaction volume \emph{also} fell sharply in exposed ZIPs (a point estimate of $-10$ log points per exposure point, $p < 0.01$ by permutation and wild bootstrap), but here identification is weaker: exposed ZIPs were on a differential downward volume path before the shutdown, the largest declines arrive in 2023, and a minor second exit (RedfinNow, 2022) does not replicate the pattern. I therefore present the volume decline as consistent with a large liquidity withdrawal but confounded by Zillow's selection into cooling markets, and bound rather than assert its causal magnitude.

These results speak to three literatures. The closest paper is \citet{buchak2025intermediating}, who ask why intermediating houses is so difficult: iBuyers buy at a 3.1 percentage-point discount, earn roughly a 5 percent gross spread, transact on algorithmizable information, and retreat from segments where pricing errors invite adverse selection. Their question is the dealer's---is machine intermediation profitable, and what caps its scale? Mine is the market's: does the machine change the quality of price discovery for the people who never trade with it? The distinction matters because the externality, not the P\&L, is what determines whether algorithmic intermediation is worth having. Reduced-form work on iBuyer entry finds price-level and volume effects \citep{harrison2024impact, seiler2023burgeoning}; I focus instead on second moments---dispersion, tails, informativeness---which is where information lives. Relative to the literature on AVMs as advisory signals---the Zestimate reduces buyer-seller uncertainty \citep{fu2025unequal}, moves transaction prices \citep{yu2020algorithmic}, and can propagate its own errors through feedback loops \citep{fu2022feedback}---the iBuyer quote is executable and capital-backed, and the exit experiment isolates precisely that difference, because the Zestimate never went away. And relative to the industrial-organization literature on algorithmic pricing, where sellers' algorithms soften competition in gasoline and rental markets \citep{calvano2020artificial, assad2024algorithmic, calderwang2026algorithmic} and machine-learning prediction redistributes surplus in credit \citep{fuster2022predictably}, the algorithm here is not pricing its owner's inventory---it is making a market, with its own capital at risk, in everyone else's.

The housing-search literature supplies the reason to expect any of this to matter. Prices in decentralized housing markets are anchors built from comparables \citep{guren2018momentum}, search is segmented \citep{piazzesi2020segmented}, liquidity is priced in both time-on-market and dispersion \citep{jiang2024liquidity}, and thick-market externalities amplify shocks \citep{ngai2014hot}. A public, firm, algorithmic quote is a new kind of signal that this literature has never had quasi-experimental variation in. This paper supplies that variation in both directions---arrival and abrupt removal---and finds that the machine's liquidity died loudly while its information died quietly: the first is visible in deed volume the quarter the algorithm stopped, the second is too small at a one-to-two-percent transaction share to rise above the noise, exactly as the model's comp-window arithmetic says it must be.

It is worth stating plainly what this paper does and does not show. It \emph{does} show, with a clean design and a tight bound, that the machine's exit did not measurably degrade price discovery for the households around it---a null the model rationalizes at the machine's one-to-two-percent reach and the data are sharp enough to make economically informative. It \emph{does not} show that algorithmic intermediation withdrew first-order liquidity: household volume fell sharply in exposed markets, but those markets were already cooling relative to controls before the shutdown, so I document the decline without certifying its cause, and I am explicit that the clean result is the information null, not the volume number. It does not identify the entry effects causally---iBuyers chose their markets, the pre-trends say so, and I treat the entry evidence as descriptive. And it speaks to the externalities of algorithmic market-making at the modest scale the industry actually reached, not at the scale a future deployment might. What survives all the caveats is a model of an algorithmic dealer with adverse selection that predicts where the machine operated, why the high-forecast-error firm was the one that failed, and how small its price-discovery footprint should be---together with the cleanest evidence to date that, at this scale, the footprint was indeed small.

\section{Institutional Background}\label{sec:institutions}

\subsection{How an instant offer works}\label{sec:inst-mechanics}

An iBuyer transaction begins with an address. The homeowner submits it through the firm's website; the firm's automated valuation model produces an estimate of the home's market value from recorded characteristics, recent comparable sales, and local price indices; and the firm returns a firm cash offer, typically within 24 to 48 hours, valid for roughly a week. The offer equals the model's estimate minus a service charge---a posted fee plus, in practice, a purchase discount that compensates the firm for holding risk, renovation, and the lemons it cannot see. \citet{buchak2025intermediating} estimate that iBuyers purchase at a 3.1 percentage-point discount relative to comparable listed homes and earn an average gross spread of about 5 percent. If the seller accepts, an inspection adjusts the price for verifiable defects, the firm closes in days on the seller's calendar, takes title through a dedicated subsidiary, performs light renovation, and relists. Roughly half of inventory resells within three months \citep{buchak2025intermediating}. Two features of this institution drive everything in this paper. First, the quote is \emph{firm}: the algorithm's owner commits capital against its own forecast, which an advisory estimate never does. Second, both legs of the trade---the machine's purchase and its resale---are recorded deeds, public information available to every appraiser, agent, and AVM in the county.

The economics of the business are a race between two numbers: the liquidity and convenience value the firm delivers to urgent sellers, which it harvests through the spread, and the valuation error of its model, which better-informed homeowners harvest through selective acceptance. A homeowner who knows the house is worth less than the quote accepts; one who knows it is worth more lists on the open market. \citet{buchak2025intermediating} show that iBuyers transact almost exclusively on algorithmizable information---observables plus ZIP-quarter effects explain over 80 percent of price variation in their purchases, against 68 percent in the broader market---and that they avoid segments where algorithmic pricing errors are large. Adverse selection is not an afterthought in this industry; it is the binding constraint, and the model in Section~\ref{sec:model} puts it at the center.

\subsection{Entry, expansion, and scale}\label{sec:inst-entry}

Opendoor invented the category. Founded in March 2014, it bought its first home in Phoenix in December 2014---the flat, tract-built, stucco-homogeneous market that would become the industry's laboratory. Offerpad followed in 2015, also headquartered in the Phoenix metro. Redfin began experimenting with RedfinNow in Southern California in 2017. Zillow, the dominant consumer real-estate platform, entered last and loudest: Zillow Offers launched in Phoenix in April 2018 and expanded to 25 metropolitan areas over the following 29 months, reaching its 25th and final market, Jacksonville, on September 8, 2020.\footnote{Zillow announced the move into direct buying on April 12, 2018 (SEC Form 8-K, Ex-99.1). Expansion dates per company press releases: Las Vegas (June 2018), Atlanta (September 2018), Denver (October 2018), Portland (July 2019), Austin (September 2019), Cincinnati (March 2, 2020, the 24th market), Jacksonville (September 8, 2020, the 25th).} All four firms paused purchases in March 2020 as COVID-19 froze the housing market---Zillow suspended buying in all markets on March 23, 2020---and all four resumed by August 2020 into the strongest sellers' market in modern history.

Scale arrived in 2021. iBuyers collectively purchased about 70{,}000 homes that year, roughly 1.3 percent of all U.S. home purchases and double the previous record. The aggregate number understates local depth, because the machines concentrated geographically exactly as adverse-selection logic predicts: in the third quarter of 2021, 12.1 percent of homes sold in Phoenix transacted through an iBuyer. Figure~\ref{fig:F1} plots the time series of iBuyer purchases in my deed data; Figure~\ref{fig:F2} shows the cross-sectional footprint. The machines bought where houses are young, similar, and easy to price---and within those metros, in the ZIP codes where the housing stock is most homogeneous.

\subsection{The collapse of Zillow Offers}\label{sec:inst-collapse}

Zillow's 2021 is the natural experiment, so its anatomy matters. Two institutional details set it up. First, in February 2021 Zillow began presenting the Zestimate itself as an initial cash offer in eligible markets---fusing its advisory signal to its dealer quote. Second, on June 15, 2021, Zillow launched the ``neural Zestimate,'' reporting a national median error rate of 1.9 percent for \emph{on-market} homes but 6.9 percent for \emph{off-market} homes. That asymmetry is the heart of the matter: an instant offer prices an off-market home, sight unseen, precisely where the algorithm's error is 3.6 times larger. The machine's public accuracy statistics advertised its strength exactly where its dealer business did not operate.

Through 2021 Zillow scaled aggressively into a decelerating market. Reporting by Insider in November 2021 described an internal initiative, ``Project Ketchup,'' launched in early 2021 to catch up with Opendoor: employees said it led Zillow to offer prices well above what its algorithm and analysts picked as market value and to buy too many homes in the summer of 2021. In the third quarter of 2021, Zillow Offers purchased 9{,}680 homes and sold 3{,}032. On October 17--18, Bloomberg reported that Zillow had stopped signing new purchase contracts for the remainder of 2021, which the company attributed to renovation and closing backlogs. On November 1, Bloomberg reported Zillow was shopping roughly 7{,}000 homes to institutional investors for about \$2.8 billion. On November 2, 2021, alongside third-quarter results, Zillow announced it would wind down Zillow Offers entirely, taking a \$304 million inventory write-down---the result, the company said, of ``purchasing homes in Q3 at higher prices than the company's current estimates of future selling prices''---with another \$240--265 million of losses expected in the fourth quarter, and a workforce reduction of approximately 25 percent. CEO Rich Barton's explanation bears quoting in full:

\begin{quote}
``We've determined the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated and continuing to scale Zillow Offers would result in too much earnings and balance-sheet volatility.''
\end{quote}

At the announcement Zillow owned 9{,}790 homes and had another 8{,}172 under contract to purchase---roughly 18{,}000 houses to liquidate. The wind-down ran through three quarters, ahead of schedule, and was complete as of September 30, 2022, when iBuying moved to discontinued operations in Zillow's financials.

Three features of this episode matter for identification. First, the failure was a failure of \emph{forecasting at the firm level}, not of demand in any particular ZIP code: the same announcement covered all 25 markets simultaneously, and Barton's stated reason was the unpredictability of home prices, amplified by a company that had overridden its own algorithm's caution. Second, the exit was \emph{selective in exactly the direction a forecast-quality story predicts}: Opendoor---whose pricing the industry regarded as sharper and whose purchase behavior \citet{buchak2025intermediating} show was disciplined by error avoidance---stayed, absorbed the same 2022 rate shock, took a \$573 million inventory write-down in the third quarter of 2022 and a \$1.353 billion net loss for fiscal 2022, and kept operating. Offerpad also stayed. RedfinNow, the smallest player, exited on November 9, 2022, a year later and for explicitly rate-driven reasons. Third, the \emph{advisory signal survived the dealer}: the Zestimate remained on every Zillow listing after November 2, 2021. What vanished from the market was the executable quote and the deed records it generated. The exit therefore isolates the quote channel from the information-display channel---the comparison the AVM literature cannot make.

\subsection{What the exit removed, by the numbers}\label{sec:inst-magnitudes}

In the ZIP codes I study, Zillow's purchases over 2018Q2--2021Q3 averaged 0.17 percent of arm's-length transactions, with the 90th percentile of exposed ZIPs at 0.45 percent and a 99th-percentile cap of 0.87 percent; the surviving machines (Opendoor and Offerpad combined) averaged 0.70 percent in the same ZIPs. Counting both sides of each intermediated house---the purchase deed and the resale deed---the machine's footprint in its better markets ran to a few percent of recorded trade. These are honest numbers to keep in view: a single buyer of one house in several hundred is a meaningful marginal bidder, but it is not the market. The empirical question is what the disappearance of a buyer of \emph{this} size---and of the public, model-generated prices its deeds put on the record---did to the remaining market, and the model disciplines both what to expect and how big it should be.

\section{A Model of Algorithmic Intermediation}\label{sec:model}

The model has two layers. The first is the machine's problem: a dealer that quotes a firm price from a noisy valuation model to sellers who know their house better than the model does. It delivers the sorting, fragility, and selective-exit results. The second layer embeds the machine's transactions in the information set of ordinary buyers and delivers the price-discovery and liquidity spillovers. Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Environment}\label{sec:model-env}

A market segment---a ZIP code, or a cell of similar houses within one---contains a continuum of houses. House $i$'s value to any buyer is $v + \varepsilon_i$, where $v$ is a segment-level common component and $\varepsilon_i \sim U[-w, w]$ is house-specific and privately observed by the seller. The half-width $w$ indexes segment heterogeneity: it is the inverse of ``machine-readability.'' In a subdivision of near-identical tract homes $w$ is small; in a neighborhood of custom houses on irregular lots it is large.

Sellers come in two types. A fraction $\lambda$ are \emph{movers}: they carry an urgency cost $D > 0$---a job in another city, a divorce, an estate---so their net payoff from a market sale is $v + \varepsilon_i - D$, and the price they negotiate in a market sale embeds a concession $\theta D$, $\theta \in (0,1]$, to the buyer who accommodates their timeline. The remaining fraction $1-\lambda$ are \emph{non-movers} with zero urgency, of whom an exogenous fraction $\nu$ list for idiosyncratic reasons and sell at fundamental value.

The machine---the algorithmic intermediary---runs an AVM that produces an estimate $\hat v = v + a$ of the segment's common value, with forecast error $a \sim U[-s, s]$, so $\sigma_a^2 = s^2/3$. It posts a firm bid
\begin{equation}
q = \hat v - m
\label{eq:quote}
\end{equation}
to any seller in the segment, where $m$ is the gross spread. It pays an all-in cost $c$ per intermediated house (transaction, holding, and renovation costs) and resells at fundamental value. The key informational asymmetry is the one the institutions dictate: the seller observes $\varepsilon_i$; the machine observes neither $\varepsilon_i$ nor its own error $a$.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:1}
$0 < c < D$; \quad $2w > D - c$; \quad $0 < s < \min\{c,\ (D-c)/2,\ 2w - (D-c)\}$.
\end{assumption}

The first condition says gains from trade exist: the machine's cost of intermediating is below the mover's urgency cost. The second says heterogeneity is not trivial relative to the liquidity gain, so the machine cannot profitably sweep the whole segment. The third bounds forecast errors by costs and by the slack in the second condition---it guarantees that no realization of the error exhausts the mover pool, keeps every threshold interior, and is the empirically relevant case: an AVM whose error rivaled the gross spread would not be deployed.

\subsection{The machine's problem}\label{sec:model-machine}

A seller accepts the quote if and only if it beats the market payoff. A mover compares $q = v + a - m$ with $v + \varepsilon_i - D$ and accepts iff $\varepsilon_i \le a - m + D$; a non-mover accepts iff $\varepsilon_i \le a - m$. Define $\mu = m - a$, the \emph{realized} spread relative to the truth, and the acceptance thresholds
\begin{equation}
\tau_\delta = a - m + \delta, \qquad \delta \in \{0, D\},
\label{eq:thresholds}
\end{equation}
for the non-mover and mover pools respectively. Acceptance is selection from below: the machine attracts the sellers whose private draws $\varepsilon_i$ are worst relative to its quote. The next lemma prices that selection exactly.

\begin{lemma}[Pool profit identity]\label{lem:pool}
For a seller pool with urgency $\delta$ and interior threshold $\tau_\delta \in [-w, w]$: the acceptance mass is $(\tau_\delta + w)/(2w)$; the mean accepted draw is $\E[\varepsilon \mid \text{accept}] = (\tau_\delta - w)/2$; and the pool's contribution to machine profit is
\begin{equation}
\frac{(\delta - c)^2 - (w + c - \mu)^2}{4w}.
\label{eq:poolprofit}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

The identity packs the whole trade-off into one difference of squares. The first square is the social surplus the pool offers---urgency net of cost. The second is the wedge between the spread the machine actually realizes, $\mu$, and the spread $w + c$ at which it would exactly break even on the marginal pool; adverse selection appears as the $w$ inside the second square. Profit from non-movers ($\delta = 0$) is $[c^2 - (w+c-\mu)^2]/4w$, which is negative whenever the non-mover pool is active ($\mu < w$): sellers with no urgency accept only when the machine has overpriced, so every non-mover trade is a lemon. The machine's problem is to set the spread wide enough to exclude them without choking off the movers.

\begin{proposition}[Machine-readable segments]\label{prop:sorting}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:1} the machine's optimal spread is $m^* = w + c$. At the optimum only movers trade: the non-mover threshold is $\tau_0 = a - w - c < -w$ for every realization of $a$, since $a \le s < c$. Expected volume is
\begin{equation}
V^* = \frac{\lambda (D - c)}{2w},
\label{eq:volume}
\end{equation}
and expected profit is
\begin{equation}
\Pi^*(w, s) = \frac{\lambda \left[ (D-c)^2 - s^2/3 \right]}{4w}.
\label{eq:profit}
\end{equation}
$V^*$ and $\Pi^*$ are strictly decreasing in $w$, and $m^*$ is strictly increasing in $w$. With a per-segment fixed operating cost $F > 0$, the machine operates if and only if
\begin{equation}
w \le \bar w = \frac{\lambda \left[ (D-c)^2 - s^2/3 \right]}{4F}.
\label{eq:wbar}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting} is the sorting result: algorithmic intermediation selects into homogeneous, machine-readable segments. The spread $m^* = w + c$ rises one-for-one with heterogeneity---the machine must charge more where it knows less---and the wider spread exactly offsets the worsening selection, leaving the per-trade margin $(D - c - a)/2$ independent of $w$; volume and profit then fall in $w$ entirely through participation, as the spread prices out more movers. With any fixed cost of standing up a market---pricing infrastructure, local renovation crews, holding facilities---the machine operates only below the heterogeneity cutoff $\bar w$. This is the cross-sectional prediction: iBuyer share should decline in the dispersion of the local housing stock, which is what \citet{buchak2025intermediating} and \citet{seiler2023burgeoning} document and what my Figure~\ref{fig:F2} shows in deeds.

\begin{proposition}[Forecast-error fragility and collapse]\label{prop:fragility}
(i) $\Pi^*$ is strictly decreasing in $s^2$:
\begin{equation}
\E_a \Pi(m^*) = \Pi(m^*; a = 0) - \frac{\lambda s^2}{12w},
\label{eq:errortax}
\end{equation}
so forecast-error variance is a pure tax on the spread. The machine operates iff
\begin{equation}
s^2 \le \bar s^2(w) = 3\left[ (D-c)^2 - \frac{4wF}{\lambda} \right],
\label{eq:sbar}
\end{equation}
and a regime shift that raises forecast-error dispersion above $\bar s$ triggers exit.
(ii) (Operational winner's curse.) Conditional on the realized error $a$, volume
\begin{equation}
V(a) = \frac{\lambda (D - c + a)}{2w}
\label{eq:condvolume}
\end{equation}
is strictly increasing in $a$ while the per-trade margin $(D - c - a)/2$ is strictly decreasing, so
\begin{equation}
\Cov(V, a) = \frac{\lambda s^2}{6w} > 0:
\label{eq:wcurse}
\end{equation}
the algorithm buys the most precisely when it overprices, and realized profit $\Pi(a) = \lambda[(D-c)^2 - a^2]/(4w)$ is lowest in its highest-volume states.
(iii) $\bar s^2(w)$ is decreasing in $w$: harder segments are more fragile.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Selective exit]\label{cor:exit}
Let firms $j$ be heterogeneous in forecast quality $s_j$, and suppose the scaled errors $\kappa s_j$ remain within Assumption~\ref{ass:1}'s bounds. A common non-stationarity shock that scales all forecast errors by $\kappa > 1$ removes exactly the operating firms with $s_j \in (\bar s/\kappa,\ \bar s]$: the high-error firm exits while the precise firm stays.
\end{corollary}

Proposition~\ref{prop:fragility} is the 2021 collapse as comparative statics. Part (i) says the machine's viability is a race between the urgency surplus $(D-c)^2$ and its error variance; nothing about local demand appears in the exit condition. Part (ii) is the mechanism reporters found in the wreckage: in mid-2021, when Zillow's model (and the human ``offer calibration'' layered on top of it) overpriced, conversion surged---9{,}680 purchases against 3{,}032 sales in a single quarter---exactly the high-$a$, high-volume, negative-margin state the covariance in \eqref{eq:wcurse} describes. The firm's own disclosure---a write-down from ``purchasing homes in Q3 at higher prices than current estimates of future selling prices''---is the winner's curse stated in accounting language. And Corollary~\ref{cor:exit} explains why the shock removed Zillow and not Opendoor: when the post-pandemic market broke the stationarity that AVMs train on, the firm whose forecast error was largest crossed the exit threshold first. I can corroborate the ordering in the deeds rather than relying on Zillow's self-published 6.9 percent off-market accuracy statistic: the within-segment dispersion of each firm's own purchase-price residuals---a direct empirical counterpart of $\sigma_a$---is largest for Zillow (0.188 log points) and smaller for Opendoor (0.174), the firm that survived. Selective exit by forecast quality is exactly the mechanism, and it is precisely what makes Opendoor-exposed ZIPs the right control group in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}.\footnote{These residual dispersions also fold in my hedonic's own error, so they overstate the pure AVM component; what the model needs is only the cross-firm \emph{ordering}, which is robust. The realized failure was surely part governance too---the ``Project Ketchup'' initiative overrode the algorithm---so I read Corollary~\ref{cor:exit} as one of two reinforcing accounts of why the high-error firm exited, not as the sole one.}

\subsection{Price discovery: the machine's quotes as public signals}\label{sec:model-discovery}

The second layer asks what the machine's presence does to everyone else's information. Buyers in household-to-household trades estimate the common value $v$ by least-squares (linear) forecasts from $k$ recent comparable transactions. A household comp is $p_j = v + \xi_j$, with $\xi_j$ i.i.d., mean zero, variance $\sigma_\xi^2$; I treat $\sigma_\xi^2$ as a primitive.\footnote{In a stationary equilibrium the comp noise $\sigma_\xi^2$ is itself a fixed point: today's buyer errors become tomorrow's comp errors, so $\sigma_\xi^2$ solves a consistency condition involving $W_B$, the bargaining variance $\sigma_b^2$, and the dispersion of $\varepsilon$ and seller-urgency concessions. Endogenizing it should amplify the comparative statics below---machine presence lowers $\sigma_\xi^2$, which lowers $W_B$ further---and I conjecture it leaves every sign intact; I work with the one-step problem and do not rely on the fixed-point for any result.}

Machine purchase prices are public deed records, and the machine's fee structure is posted; de-spreading a machine deed by the known $m^*$ yields the signal $\hat v = v + a$, with variance $\sigma_a^2$. Crucially, this error is \emph{common to all machine comps in the segment}: one model, one error. Buyers always harvest their $k$ household comps, scanning back as far as needed; a machine deed among the most recent transactions arrives \emph{on top of} the household comps, not in place of one. Buying-side information therefore depends on whether the recent record contains a machine trade. With $v_m$ denoting the machine's share of segment transactions, the probability that the window of the $k$ most recent transactions contains at least one machine deed is
\begin{equation}
\rho(v_m) = 1 - (1 - v_m)^k,
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
strictly increasing and concave in $v_m$: even a modest machine share seeds a large fraction of comp windows. The projection MSEs are
\begin{equation}
V_0 = \frac{\sigma_\xi^2}{k}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
V_1 = \left( \frac{k}{\sigma_\xi^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_a^2} \right)^{-1}
\label{eq:mses}
\end{equation}
without and with the machine signal. Transaction prices in household trades are
\begin{equation}
p_i = \hat v_i + \varepsilon_i - \theta D \cdot \ind{\text{mover}} + b_i,
\label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
where $\hat v_i$ is the buyer's estimate of $v$ and $b_i \sim (0, \sigma_b^2)$ is bargaining noise. The price loads one-for-one on $\varepsilon_i$ because a matched buyer inspects the house: inspection reveals the house-specific component---condition, layout, the lot---while what it cannot reveal is the segment's common value, which is exactly the object the comps estimate and the machine's model prices.

\begin{proposition}[Price discovery]\label{prop:discovery}
The buyer-error component of within-segment dispersion of household-to-household prices around fundamentals is
\begin{equation}
W_B(\rho) = \rho \, \frac{V_1^2 k}{\sigma_\xi^2} + (1 - \rho) V_0 + \rho (1 - \rho) \frac{V_1^2}{\sigma_a^2},
\label{eq:WB}
\end{equation}
where the last term is the across-group mean gap: buyers with a machine comp share the common error $a$, sitting at $\mathrm{gap} = (V_1/\sigma_a^2)\, a$ from buyers without one. The derivative $dW_B/d\rho$ is linear in $\rho$ and negative at both endpoints---it equals $V_1 - V_0 < 0$ at $\rho = 0$ and $V_1 - V_0 - 2V_1^2/\sigma_a^2 < 0$ at $\rho = 1$---hence $W_B$ is strictly decreasing in $\rho$, and therefore in $v_m$. Consequently:
(i) machine presence compresses price dispersion among trades the machine is \emph{not} part of;
(ii) exit raises dispersion by $\rho(v_m^{pre}) \left( V_0 - V_1 + \text{cross-terms} \right)$, strictly increasing in the pre-exit machine share.
\end{proposition}

The economics is worth stating plainly. The machine's deeds inject a signal whose error is common rather than idiosyncratic. A buyer who sees one machine comp learns a lot ($V_1 < V_0$); but as machine comps spread, buyers increasingly share the \emph{same} mistake $a$, which adds a between-group variance term $\rho(1-\rho)V_1^2/\sigma_a^2$. The proposition shows the first force wins everywhere: dispersion falls monotonically in machine share. The common error never undoes the gain---it caps it. This is the Glosten--Milgrom intuition in a search market: the dealer's quotes do the market's information aggregation, and third parties free-ride.

A second, compositional channel works in the same direction. By Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting}, the machine absorbs movers with low $\varepsilon_i$---sellers who, in its absence, would have transacted at discounted prices in the household market. Removing them truncates the household pool from below, mechanically compressing measured dispersion. The two channels are distinguishable in data: truncation moves the tails and is invisible to a robust scale measure, while information moves the whole distribution. I therefore measure dispersion both ways---standard deviation and IQR-based---and read their divergence as the composition share of the effect.

\subsection{Liquidity and the left tail}\label{sec:model-liquidity}

\begin{proposition}[Liquidity]\label{prop:liquidity}
(i) Recorded transaction volume without the machine is $V_{0,total} = \lambda + (1 - \lambda)\nu$, and rises one-for-one with machine purchases: an intermediated house generates two deeds---machine purchase plus machine resale---against one in the counterfactual. Exit removes this flow in proportion to pre-exit exposure.
(ii) (Fire sales.) With $b = 0$ and buyers pricing at the true common value ($\hat v_i = v$), household trades at more than $t$ below fundamental, for any $t \le \theta D$, are exactly mover sales. The machine absorbs movers with $\varepsilon \le \tau_D$, so the fire-sale share of household trades falls from
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda + (1-\lambda)\nu}
\qquad \text{to} \qquad
\frac{\lambda \, P(\varepsilon > \tau_D)}{\lambda \, P(\varepsilon > \tau_D) + (1-\lambda)\nu}.
\label{eq:firesale}
\end{equation}
Exit reverses this.
\end{proposition}

The machine is a liquidity provider in the textbook sense of \citet{duffie2005otc} and \citet{weill2007leaning}: it stands ready to buy from the agents for whom waiting is most expensive. Its presence shows up in deeds as more volume and a thinner left tail of prices; its exit shows up as the reverse, concentrated where it had been buying.

\subsection{Testable predictions}\label{sec:model-predictions}

The model maps to the two research designs as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{P1 (sorting; Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting}).} Cross-section: iBuyer share declines in ZIP-level housing-stock heterogeneity. [Figure~\ref{fig:F2}; Table~\ref{tab:T4} interactions.]
\item \textbf{P2 (fragility; Proposition~\ref{prop:fragility}, Corollary~\ref{cor:exit}).} Time series: a non-stationarity shock removes the highest-error algorithm while precise rivals stay---the 2021 collapse narrative of Section~\ref{sec:inst-collapse}. The winner's curse (high volume in overpricing states) is Zillow's Q3 2021.
\item \textbf{P3 (price discovery; Proposition~\ref{prop:discovery}).} Entry event studies: within-ZIP dispersion of non-iBuyer prices falls after entry. Exit DiD: dispersion rises after November 2021 in proportion to Zillow exposure, against an Opendoor-exposure placebo; SD-based and IQR-based measures separate information from composition.
\item \textbf{P4 (liquidity; Proposition~\ref{prop:liquidity}).} Entry: volume up, left-tail share down. Exit: volume down, left-tail share up, in proportion to Zillow exposure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Deeds and housing characteristics}\label{sec:data-deeds}

The transaction data are CoreLogic deed records (Owner Transfer), the standard near-universe of recorded U.S. property transfers, merged to CoreLogic Property Characteristics at the parcel level. I use the 22 states in which at least one iBuyer operated, from 2010Q1 through 2024Q2---a window that brackets the industry's birth, peak, and partial death. I retain market sales: arm's-length category-A deeds; residential single-family and condominium parcels (property indicator 10/11); prices between \$10{,}000 and \$10 million; excluding inter-family transfers, foreclosure and REO-related deeds. The filter waterfall is in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:waterfall}; the final sample contains 42{,}382{,}121 arm's-length transactions across 18{,}657 state-ZIP units in 525 core-based statistical areas.

\subsection{Identifying the machines in deeds}\label{sec:data-ibuyers}

iBuyers transact through dedicated subsidiaries whose names appear as the first-listed buyer or seller on the deed. I identify them with curated entity-name patterns: OPENDOOR (including the ``OD Arizona/Nevada/Texas LLC'' family), ZILLOW (in practice, Zillow Homes Property Trust), OFFERPAD (including the ``OP SPE'' family---OP SPE PHX1 LLC and siblings), and REDFINNOW (RedfinNow Borrower LLC). The patterns are verified against each firm's SEC Exhibit 21.1 subsidiary lists, so the match is built on disclosed legal entities rather than string guesswork; Appendix~\ref{app:data} reports the full pattern table. A national scan of the deed data yields 376{,}256 iBuyer-linked deeds: 193{,}054 purchases and 185{,}635 dispositions. Three external checks validate the match. The 2021 purchase count in my data is 67{,}626 against industry trackers' estimate of roughly 70{,}000 nationally. The mean hedonic residual on iBuyer purchases is $-2.9$ log points---independently reproducing the 3.1-percentage-point purchase discount that \citet{buchak2025intermediating} document in different data---while dispositions resell at $+0.8$ log points, the spread the business model implies. And the purchase-to-disposition pairing reproduces the institutional timeline: short holding spells, concentrated metros, the 2021 Zillow surge and the 2021Q4--2022 liquidation.

\subsection{Outcomes}\label{sec:data-outcomes}

All outcomes are at the ZIP5-by-quarter level and built from non-iBuyer (``household'') trades; I am explicit below about where the machine's own deeds enter.

\emph{Volume.} My primary volume outcome is the log count of \emph{household-to-household} arm's-length transactions---the machine's purchases and resales netted out of the cell count. This matters for the exit design: a cell's gross transaction count mechanically contains the machine's disappearing deeds, so part of any exit coefficient on gross volume is subtraction rather than economic contraction. Netting them out isolates the trade among parties the machine was never one of. I report the gross-volume version alongside for transparency.

\emph{Dispersion.} I estimate a yearly hedonic regression per state---log price on log square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, age, age squared, a condo indicator, and ZIP fixed effects---on non-iBuyer trades only, and take residuals. Bedrooms and bathrooms enter with missing-value indicators: assessors in some states (Arizona and Maryland among them) do not report room counts, and the hedonic must not discard whole states over one regressor. Within-cell dispersion is the standard deviation of these residuals, and, separately, the IQR divided by 1.349 (the normal-consistent robust scale). The SD responds to tails; the IQR-based measure does not; Proposition~\ref{prop:discovery} says their divergence measures the composition channel. Cells require at least five household trades.

\emph{Left tail.} I report two versions. The fire-sale margin of Proposition~\ref{prop:liquidity}(ii) is the share of household trades priced below fundamentals, which I measure as the share with \emph{raw} hedonic residual below $-0.25$ (roughly 22 percent below the price characteristics predict). I also report the share below $-0.25$ in the within-cell \emph{demeaned} residual, which is a dispersion-tail rather than a below-predicted-price tail; the two coincide only when the cell's mean residual is zero. The text is explicit about which is which.

\emph{Median price.} The within-cell median log price, used to check that information effects are not mislabeled price-level effects.

\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

The two designs are deliberately asymmetric. Entry was chosen; exit was forced. I use the entry design to establish the joint pattern the model predicts and the exit design to identify the information externality.

\subsection{Design 1: staggered entry}\label{sec:strategy-entry}

I define a CBSA's entry cohort as the first quarter with at least ten iBuyer purchases---a threshold that separates operational launch from stray test purchases. The estimation panel is ZIP-by-quarter, 2012Q1--2021Q3, ending before the Zillow shutdown so the entry design is uncontaminated by the exit. Because treatment timing is staggered and effects are plausibly dynamic and heterogeneous, two-way fixed effects is unreliable \citep{goodmanbacon2021difference, dechaisemartin2020two}; I estimate interaction-weighted event studies following \citet{sun2021estimating}:
\begin{equation}
y_{zt} = \sum_{e \neq -1} \beta_e \, \ind{t - G_{c(z)} = e} + \alpha_z + \delta_t + \epsilon_{zt},
\label{eq:entry}
\end{equation}
where $G_{c(z)}$ is the entry quarter of ZIP $z$'s CBSA, the $\beta_e$ are interaction-weighted cohort averages, never-entered CBSAs serve as controls, and standard errors are clustered by CBSA. The estimator belongs to the family of heterogeneity-robust designs developed by \citet{callaway2021difference} and \citet{borusyak2024revisiting}; in robustness I add state-by-quarter fixed effects, weight by the residential stock, compare against the static two-way fixed-effects coefficient, reassign treatment one quarter early, and end the panel before COVID.

Entry is endogenous: iBuyers selected flat, liquid, homogeneous markets on observables---that is Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting}, and it is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:F2}. The design therefore leans on timing rather than levels: the identifying assumption is that, conditional on ZIP and quarter effects, the quarter in which a metro's algorithmic buyer turned on is not correlated with counterfactual trends in second moments. Three threats deserve attention. First, selection on trends rather than levels---addressed by showing flat pre-event coefficients over a three-year window. Second, anticipation: entries were announced weeks, not years, ahead, and I probe sensitivity by reassigning treatment one quarter early. Third, COVID: the pause and restart of 2020 are absorbed by calendar-time effects common to treated and control metros, and results are robust to ending the panel in 2019Q4.

\subsection{Design 2: the Zillow exit}\label{sec:strategy-exit}

The exit design compares ZIP codes by their exposure to the disappeared machine, within metro and quarter. Exposure is
\begin{equation}
\text{Zshare}_z = 100 \times \frac{\text{Zillow purchases in } z, \ 2018\text{Q}2\text{--}2021\text{Q}3}{\text{total arm's-length sales in } z, \ 2018\text{Q}2\text{--}2021\text{Q}3},
\label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
winsorized at the 99th percentile. The control loading is the same object for the surviving machines: $\text{ODshare}_z$, the combined Opendoor-plus-Offerpad share over the same window. The sample is ZIPs with at least 50 transactions in the exposure window and at least 500 residential parcels, in CBSAs with at least 50 Zillow purchases---markets where Zillow Offers genuinely operated---over 2019Q1--2024Q2; ZIPs in those CBSAs with zero Zillow exposure are the within-CBSA controls. The specification is
\begin{equation}
y_{zt} = \beta \, \text{Zshare}_z \times \text{Post}_t + \gamma \, \text{ODshare}_z \times \text{Post}_t + \alpha_z + \delta_{c(z) \times t} + e_{zt},
\label{eq:exit}
\end{equation}
with $\text{Post}_t = \ind{t \ge 2021\text{Q}4}$, ZIP fixed effects, CBSA-by-quarter fixed effects, and clustering by CBSA. The event-study version replaces $\text{Post}_t$ with quarter interactions, omitting 2021Q3.

Identification rests on the institutional argument of Section~\ref{sec:inst-collapse}: the shutdown was driven by the failure of a national forecasting system---unpredictability that ``far exceeds what we anticipated,'' in the CEO's words---not by conditions in any particular ZIP. The threat is not that Zillow chose its exit timing on local trends; it is that Zillow-exposed ZIPs differ from other ZIPs in ways that matter after 2021. The ODshare control is built for exactly that: Opendoor and Offerpad selected ZIPs on the same machine-readability criteria (Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting} applies to all of them), so $\gamma$ absorbs the ``iBuyer-attractive ZIP'' selection, and---because the surviving machines' markets took the 2022 rate shock too---it absorbs the Sunbelt cooling that coincided with the post period. $\beta$ is then identified from variation in \emph{which} machine a ZIP was exposed to, under the assumption that, conditional on metro-quarter effects and overall iBuyer loading, Zillow-tilted ZIPs would have trended like Opendoor-tilted ZIPs absent the shutdown.

The information and liquidity outcomes meet that assumption to very different degrees, and I am explicit about which result I stake the paper on. The \emph{information} outcomes pass their falsification tests cleanly: within-ZIP price dispersion shows no differential pre-trend (a fake-2020Q3 placebo permutation centers on zero, $p = 0.56$), so the post-shutdown null is identified. The \emph{volume} outcome does not: exposed ZIPs---the ones Zillow bought into heavily during its 2021 ramp---were already on a differential downward volume path before the shutdown (the same placebo rejects, $p = 0.01$), and the largest declines arrive in 2023, two years late. A balance table (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:T6}) shows exposure groups differ on stock composition, as the sorting model requires, but are balanced on pre-period appreciation; the surviving-machine loading is a clean placebo; and inference is robust to the 27-cluster design (wild cluster bootstrap with Webb weights, 999-draw within-CBSA permutation). But none of this repairs a pre-trend in the volume outcome itself. I therefore treat the price-discovery null as the identified contribution and the volume decline as suggestive but confounded by Zillow's selection into cooling markets---a distinction the data force, and one I do not paper over.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Entry}\label{sec:results-entry}

Table~\ref{tab:T2} reports the entry estimates; Figure~\ref{fig:F3} plots the event studies. The entry design returns exactly the verdict its endogeneity warnings predict, and I report it without causal varnish. Median prices rise 1.2 log points after entry (s.e.\ 0.4), but on a pre-trend that rejects flatness at $p < 0.001$: the machines chased appreciating markets, and no event-study reweighting undoes that. Volume \emph{falls} by 2.2 log points (s.e.\ 1.3, pre-trend $p = 0.05$)---the opposite of Proposition~\ref{prop:liquidity}(i)'s mechanical arithmetic---which reads as the inventory tightening of the boom markets the machines selected, not as an effect of entry. The SD-based dispersion measure shows nothing ($+0.15$ log points, s.e.\ 0.27) and fails its own pre-trend test ($p = 0.01$). The single outcome whose pre-trends are clean is the robust IQR-based dispersion measure (joint $p = 0.77$): it falls by 0.39 log points after entry (s.e.\ 0.22, $p = 0.08$), the model-consistent sign, and the sign survives all five variants in Table~\ref{tab:T2} (estimates $-0.26$ to $-0.63$ log points). Selection visible everywhere, one clean outcome pointing the model's way: that is what the entry data contain, and it is why identification rests on the exit. The estimation sample is 264{,}485 ZIP-quarters for dispersion outcomes and 320{,}667 for volume and prices.

\subsection{Exit}\label{sec:results-exit}

Table~\ref{tab:T3} reports estimates of equation~\eqref{eq:exit}; Figure~\ref{fig:F4} plots the event studies. I lead with the cleanly identified result---the price-discovery null---and treat the volume decline, which is large but confounded, second.

\emph{Price discovery.} When the machine's quotes vanished, within-ZIP dispersion of household prices did not measurably rise. The SD-based estimate is $+0.84$ log points per percentage point of Zillow exposure (s.e.\ $1.06$, permutation $p = 0.44$); the composition-free IQR measure---the one my own framework (Section~\ref{sec:model-discovery}) designates as the information channel---is $-0.20$ (s.e.\ $0.63$); the raw fire-sale tail moves $+0.39$ percentage points (s.e.\ $0.67$). Every estimate is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and the null is \emph{identified}: the fake-2020Q3 placebo permutation centers on zero for dispersion ($p = 0.56$) and the tail ($p = 0.22$), so there is no differential pre-trend masking an effect. The bound is economically tight. At mean exposure the IQR point estimate is a fraction of a log point against a baseline of $31$, and the 95 percent one-sided ceiling is $0.6$ percent of baseline dispersion. Median prices in exposed ZIPs show $+1.5$ log points (s.e.\ $1.8$): no differential price-level movement either. Section~\ref{sec:interpretation} shows this ceiling is sharp enough to reject the model's most aggressive calibrations and to confirm its central one---a signal in under two percent of comparable-sale windows cannot move price discovery much. The dispersion sample is $49{,}130$ ZIP-quarters; the extensive-margin check (Table~\ref{tab:T7}, Panel C) finds a small, positive treatment association with cell inclusion ($+0.024$, $p = 0.02$)---the opposite sign from the attrition that would bias the null toward zero, so the null is not a selection artifact.

\emph{Volume.} Household-to-household volume---the machine's own deeds netted out---falls by $10.0$ log points per percentage point of Zillow exposure (s.e.\ $3.5$; permutation $p = 0.01$; wild cluster bootstrap $p = 0.01$), while the surviving-machine loading is a precise zero ($-0.5$ log points, s.e.\ $0.7$); the household-only estimate is, if anything, larger than the gross-volume version (Table~\ref{tab:T3}, row 2), so the decline is not the machine's own disappearing deeds. At mean exposure that is a $1.7$ percent decline; at the 90th percentile, $4.5$ percent. But I do not claim this as a clean causal effect, and the event study (Figure~\ref{fig:F4}) shows why. Exposed ZIPs---the ones Zillow bought into during its 2021 ramp---carry positive relative volume in 2019 ($+8$ to $+11$ log points) that drifts down toward the 2021Q3 reference, and the fake-2020Q3 placebo rejects ($p = 0.01$): these markets were already on a differential downward path before the shutdown. The post-2021Q4 coefficients step down ($-7$, then $-13$ log points in 2022Q1) but bounce back by mid-2022 and reach their largest values only in 2023---a profile more consistent with the cooling of Sunbelt markets Zillow had specifically concentrated in than with a clean break at the announcement. The dose-response (Table~\ref{tab:T7}, Panel B) is significant at every exposure tercile and roughly flat ($-7.3$, $-6.5$, $-6.4$ log points), which rules out a handful of extreme-dose ZIPs driving the slope but also shows the response is closer to an extensive ``any-Zillow'' margin than a linear dose. And the vintage split (Panel A) is uninformative for the cleanest test: Zillow's pre-2021 purchasing in these ZIPs was negligible (the pre-Ketchup exposure mean is essentially zero), so almost all the identifying variation is the 2021 ramp itself---exactly the froth-era buying whose reversion I cannot rule out. The honest conclusion is that the volume decline is large and robust to the wind-down (the donut estimate is $-8.7$, s.e.\ $3.6$) but not cleanly separable from Zillow's selection into cooling markets.

A second, smaller event sharpens the caution rather than dispelling it. RedfinNow---a minor iBuyer that never ramped in 2021---shut down in November 2022; its exit shows no volume effect ($+0.1$ log points per point of exposure, s.e.\ $2.8$, in $14$ CBSAs). The liquidity-multiplier reading predicts a scaled-down replication and does not get one; whether that reflects RedfinNow's tiny footprint (mean exposure under a tenth of a percent, leaving the test underpowered) or the absence of a genuine causal channel, the data cannot say. Taken together, the exit evidence \emph{identifies} a tight null on price discovery and \emph{documents} a large but confounded volume decline.

\subsection{Heterogeneity by machine-readability}\label{sec:results-hetero}

Proposition~\ref{prop:discovery} ties the size of the information spillover to the machine's local share, and Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting} ties that share to stock homogeneity. The sorting itself is sharp in the data: Figure~\ref{fig:F2} shows the iBuyer transaction share falling by 1.28 percentage points per unit of stock-heterogeneity (the SD of log square footage) across 8{,}509 ZIPs---the machines went where the machines could read. But sorting (a cross-section in the share the machine \emph{achieves}) and spillover heterogeneity (how the exit \emph{effect} varies across segments) are different objects, and the second is not resolvable at these effect sizes. Table~\ref{tab:T4} splits the designs by readability tercile; the estimates are uniformly insignificant. With the average spillover bounded near zero, its cross-segment pattern is below the design's power, and I report the splits for completeness rather than as a test of Proposition~\ref{prop:discovery}.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:results-robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:T5} collects the full robustness grid for the two primary outcomes, with the Opendoor/Offerpad placebo loading near zero throughout. The \emph{magnitude} of the household-volume estimate is stable---dropping the liquidation quarters ($-10.2$ log points), ZIP-level clustering ($-10.0$), transaction weighting ($-9.3$), 95th-percentile winsorization ($-9.8$), a ten-trade cell minimum ($-11.1$), and excluding Phoenix ($-9.5$) all leave it between roughly $-9$ and $-11$, with permutation and wild-bootstrap $p$-values below 0.05; only the top-quartile binary coarsens it ($-2.2$), consistent with the flat tercile dose-response. I stress again that this stability is stability of a \emph{magnitude} whose causal interpretation the pre-trend clouds, not a robustness that resolves the confound. The price-discovery bound is stable in the sense that matters for it: every cell of the grid leaves the dispersion effect between $0.1$ and $1.6$ log points and statistically indistinguishable from zero. For the descriptive entry design, the IQR-dispersion estimate is $-0.31$ log points reassigning treatment a quarter early and $-0.63$ (s.e.\ $0.20$) ending the panel in 2019Q4 to excise COVID; the columns of Table~\ref{tab:T2} bracket the baseline.

\section{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}

\subsection{Magnitudes: the information value of a firm quote}\label{sec:interp-magnitudes}

Start with the result the design identifies: the price-discovery bound. Read off the composition-free IQR measure, the exit effect is $-0.20$ log points per exposure point and statistically zero; the SD measure is $+0.84$ and equally insignificant; both have clean pre-period placebos. Rather than dollarize a point estimate indistinguishable from zero, I state a ceiling: the data rule out an information externality larger than about $0.6$ percent of baseline dispersion at the achieved scale (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). What makes this bound economically meaningful is that the model, fed its inputs from data rather than assumptions, predicts an effect in the same small range. Zillow's deed flow at mean exposure is $v_m \approx 0.3$ percent of segment transactions; with comp windows of $k\in\{4,6,8\}$, equation~\eqref{eq:rho} gives the quote a reach of $\rho$ around one to two percent of buyers. The signal's error $\sigma_a$ I take not from a free parameter but from Zillow's \emph{own} within-segment purchase-price residual dispersion in my deeds, $0.188$ in log units---above the roughly ten-percent standard deviation implied by Zillow's published 6.9 percent median off-market error, because it also absorbs my hedonic's own error, so it is a conservative (large) input. Feeding these into Proposition~\ref{prop:discovery}, the predicted dispersion effect of the exit ranges up to $0.45$ log points across the parameter grid (Table~\ref{tab:calibration})---and here the bound has teeth: the data's IQR ceiling, $0.17$ log points at mean exposure, lies \emph{below} the model's most aggressive cells (large comp windows and a high buyer-error share), so the design actually rejects them, while it is consistent with the model's central calibrations. The honest reading is therefore neither ``confirmed'' nor ``no power'': the externality is real in the model and bounded tightly in the data, and the data are sharp enough to say it was, at most, a fraction of a percent of baseline pricing dispersion. A public signal reaching one comparable-sale window in fifty cannot move segment-level price discovery much, and it did not.

The volume decline is the larger number but the weaker claim, and I treat it accordingly. At mean exposure the exit is associated with a $1.7$ percent fall in household trade, and the estimate survives netting out the machine's own deeds, dropping the wind-down, and the full robustness grid. Were it clean, the natural reading would be that the firm quote was an outside option that made marginal listings happen---a homeowner with a guaranteed cash exit lists and negotiates differently than one without---amplified by the thin-market externality of \citet{ngai2014hot}; and the model would organize the sign and incidence while leaving the multiplier (Proposition~\ref{prop:liquidity}(i) predicts only the machine's mechanical flow) to future structural work on the listing margin. But the event study and placebo say the estimate is not clean: exposed ZIPs were cooling relative to controls before the shutdown, and the largest declines come two years later, so an unknown share of the $1.7$ percent is the reversion of the 2021 boom Zillow had chased rather than the loss of its standing bid. I therefore offer the liquidity channel as a hypothesis the data are consistent with, not a result the design establishes---and note that distinguishing the two would take an instrument for exposure that is orthogonal to local price trajectories, which the iBuyer setting does not naturally provide.

The comparison with the equities microstructure literature is then instructive precisely in the negative. \citet{hendershott2011algorithmic} found that algorithmic trading narrowed spreads and made quotes more informative; \citet{brogaard2014hft} found that high-frequency traders trade in the direction of permanent price changes. One might have expected the first market-making capital housing ever had to do the same for price discovery. It did not---not because algorithmic quotes are uninformative, but because of scale: in a limit-order book the algorithms \emph{are} the price, while in housing the machine wrote one deed in several hundred, reached one comp window in fifty, and its removal left the price system statistically undisturbed. The open question the episode poses is what the externality would be at ten times the share, where $\rho(v_m)$ approaches one-fifth of comp windows and, by the same calibration, the model's predicted effect rises into the range the data could detect. That is the experiment a future at-scale deployment would run; the 2021 episode ran a smaller one, and its answer for price discovery is a well-measured almost-nothing.

\subsection{Welfare and fragility}\label{sec:interp-welfare}

The welfare reading must respect the asymmetry in what the data establish. The cleanest entry is a \emph{non-}finding with a tight bound: at the scale the industry reached, the machine's quotes produced no measurable price-discovery externality, positive or negative, for the households around it---the information commons one might have feared losing was, on the evidence, never large. That is reassuring for the bystanders and sobering for the technology's boosters in equal measure. The second entry, liquidity, is where the welfare stakes plausibly lie---a standing algorithmic bid is exactly the outside option thin housing markets lack---but it is also where my identification is weakest, so I can document a large volume decline without certifying its surplus consequences; pinning them down is the first-order task for future work with cleaner exposure variation. The third entry, fragility, the model delivers even though the experiment cannot fully adjudicate it: Proposition~\ref{prop:fragility} says an institution that provides a market's marginal liquidity (and, at larger scale, its price signal) is one regime shift from removing both, and Corollary~\ref{cor:exit}---corroborated by Zillow's larger deed-level forecast-error dispersion---says the removal comes precisely when forecasting is hardest. A market that leans on one private algorithm inherits that algorithm's failure modes. This is the \citet{grossman1980impossibility} tension with a balance sheet attached, and it is the durable lesson of the episode regardless of how the liquidity magnitude is eventually resolved.

The concentration point deserves emphasis, with a caveat about scale. Price discovery in equities is produced by thousands of competing algorithms; the housing market's brief experiment ran on three or four, of which one---the one with the larger forecast error---was the second-largest. The instinct that this concentration made the market fragile to the algorithm's failure is right about the mechanism but, on this evidence, wrong about the magnitude for \emph{price discovery}: the machine was simply too small a share of the comparable-sale record for its exit to register. The concentration that matters is prospective. If algorithmic market-making returns at ten times the scale, the same calibration that rationalizes today's null implies a price-discovery externality the market would feel, produced by a handful of private models trained on a common stationarity---and removed together when that stationarity breaks.

\subsection{Policy}\label{sec:interp-policy}

Two regulatory margins follow directly. First, AVM oversight: bank regulators have moved toward quality-control standards for automated valuation models used in lending. The model enlarges the stakes---AVM errors need not stay inside the firm that makes them. When a model's output is an executable quote whose deed becomes a public comp, forecast error propagates into the information environment of every neighboring transaction, a feedback the Zestimate literature has documented in advisory form \citep{fu2022feedback} and that the firm-quote channel amplifies with capital. Second, appraisal waivers: the GSEs increasingly waive in-person appraisals in favor of model-based collateral assessment in exactly the homogeneous, machine-readable segments where iBuyers operated (Proposition~\ref{prop:sorting} describes both selections). The exit experiment is a caution about correlated failure: if both the market's marginal buyer and its collateral assessor are statistical models trained on the same stationarity, the states of the world in which one fails are the states in which both do.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

For roughly seven years, the largest decentralized asset market in the world ran an experiment it did not know it was running. Statistical models, backed by corporate balance sheets, posted firm prices on hundreds of thousands of American homes---the first market-making capital that housing has ever had. This paper asked what that did to price discovery for everyone else, and used the abrupt, forecasting-driven death of the second-largest machine to find out.

The answer, stated honestly, is mostly a well-measured null. When the machine's quotes vanished, the price discovery of the households around it did not measurably suffer: within-ZIP dispersion held steady, the estimate is statistically zero with a clean pre-period placebo, and the bound is tight enough to reject the larger effects one might have feared. A model of algorithmic intermediation explains why---the machine reached one comparable-sale window in fifty, too small a share of the record to move segment-level price discovery---and the same model fed Zillow's own deed-level forecast errors predicts an effect inside the bound. Household transaction volume \emph{did} fall sharply in exposed markets, hinting that the machine's standing bid had supported trade among third parties; but those markets were cooling before the shutdown, so I document that decline without claiming it cleanly, and leave its causal magnitude to a design with exposure variation orthogonal to local price trends. The theory carries more than the reduced form does: it organizes where the machines operated (homogeneous, machine-readable segments, visibly so in the deeds), and why the high-forecast-error firm was the one that died (the operational winner's curse that Zillow's final quarter exhibited almost line by line, with its larger deed-level error confirmed against the surviving rival's). The Zestimate, the advisory signal, never blinked throughout.

The broader lesson is about the industrial organization of information in decentralized markets, and it is more measured than the boosters or the critics of AI in housing would have it. A statistical dealer with real capital at risk is a genuinely new institution, and at the scale this one reached it left the price system almost exactly as it found it: no measurable sharpening, no measurable degradation, a public-good externality bounded to near zero. That is itself a finding---the first deployment of algorithmic market-making in the largest consumer asset market did not, on the evidence, change how well that market discovered prices. What the model insists, and what the episode cannot refute, is that this verdict is a function of \emph{scale}: at ten times the share the same arithmetic delivers an externality the market would feel, produced by a few private models trained on a common stationarity and apt to fail together when it breaks. The 2021 collapse ran the small version of that experiment and returned a quiet answer. The next deployment may run the large one, and it may not announce itself as politely.
