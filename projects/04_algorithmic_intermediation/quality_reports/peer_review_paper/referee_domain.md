# Referee Report — Referee A (Domain)

**Paper:** "The Machine in the Market: Algorithmic Intermediation and Price Discovery in the Housing Market"
**Journal:** Quarterly Journal of Economics
**Disposition:** STRUCTURAL | **Critical peeve applied:** parameter plausibility for calibration claims | **Constructive peeve applied:** honest bounding of claims
**Date:** 2026-06-11

## Summary

The paper asks whether iBuyers — the first agents to post firm, capital-backed, algorithmic prices in U.S. housing — improved liquidity and price discovery for third parties. It builds a two-layer adverse-selection model (a machine dealer that sorts into homogeneous segments, faces forecast-error variance as a tax on its spread, suffers an operational winner's curse, and whose public deed prices compress third-party dispersion), then tests the spillovers using the November 2021 Zillow Offers shutdown, comparing Zillow-exposed ZIPs to ZIPs exposed to the surviving machines within metro-quarter cells. The identified finding is a liquidity externality: volume fell 8.0 log points per percentage point of Zillow exposure (permutation p = 0.03), several times the machine's deed footprint. The information externality — the question in the title — is a statistically zero 0.84 log points, which Section 7 argues is "exactly" what the model predicts at the machine's ~0.3 percent share. The exit design is genuinely clever, the deed work is careful, and the paper's honesty about its own weak spots is well above the norm. But the model's quantitative predictions — which are load-bearing for the paper's interpretation of both headline numbers — are held to asymmetric standards: the calibration that "explains" the dispersion null is undisciplined and, as constructed, unrejectable; and the model's one sharp quantitative prediction (volume falls one-for-one with the machine's flow) is violated by a factor of roughly eight and rescued by an amplification mechanism imported from outside the model.

## Major concerns

### 1. The Section 7.1 calibration is undisciplined, internally inconsistent, and — as constructed — cannot fail

The claim that the dispersion bound "sits exactly where the model puts it" rests on three free parameters with zero citations: k = 6 comps, V1 = V0/2, and buyer error equal to one-third of residual variance. Work through what these jointly imply. With baseline residual SD of 40.9 log points and a buyer-error share of 1/3, the buyer's common-value error SD is sqrt(0.409^2/3) ~ 24 log points. V1 = V0/2 then forces sigma_a = sqrt(V0) ~ 24 log points — i.e., the calibration implicitly assumes the machine's de-spread signal carries a 24 percent error, 2.4 times Zillow's own published off-market error (6.9 percent median ~ 10 percent SD), which the paper itself quotes in Section 2.3. The parameters are not just unsourced; they contradict the paper's own institutional evidence.

Worse, the exercise has no power against any alternative by construction. At ZIP-level v_m ~ 0.3 percent and k = 6, rho ~ 1.8 percent of comp windows. Push every parameter to its most generous extreme — buyer error 100 percent of residual variance, V1 -> 0 — and the model's predicted exit effect is ~ 0.9 percent of baseline dispersion, still inside the 1.2 percent CI upper bound. There is *no* calibration of this model, at this measured exposure, that the data could reject. "The estimate and the model agree" is therefore not a successful quantitative test; it is a demonstration that the test cannot fail. The CI also contains zero and negative effects of equal magnitude. The bound becomes informative only through rho — and rho depends on two things the paper never interrogates: the comp-window size k and, critically, the spatial granularity at which v_m is measured. The model's own segment definition is "a ZIP code, *or a cell of similar houses within one*," and the sorting result says the machine concentrated within ZIPs in machine-readable subdivisions, where local v_m (and comps drawn from the same subdivision) could be 5-10x the ZIP mean — at which point plausible calibrations land *above* the CI bound and the test acquires teeth.

**What would change my mind:** A calibration table with sources for every parameter: k from GSE/appraisal-practice norms; sigma_a pinned from data you already have — the within-cell dispersion of Zillow's own purchase-price residuals in your deeds, cross-checked against Zillow's published off-market error; the buyer-error share bounded from the repeat-sales decomposition literature (e.g., Giacoletti) rather than asserted. Then: (i) a power statement — the set of (rho, buyer-error-share) pairs the CI rejects; (ii) exposure recomputed at finer geography (tract or within-ZIP concentration) so rho reflects the machine's actual local comp density; and (iii) replacement of "sits exactly where the model puts it" with the defensible one-sided claim: the data rule out an information externality larger than ~1.2 percent of baseline dispersion at achieved scale, consistent with — but not confirmatory of — the model.

### 2. The paper's own SD-vs-IQR decomposition contradicts the Section 7.1 reading

Section 3.3 (and the discussion after Proposition 3) stakes out a measurement identity: information moves the whole distribution (SD *and* IQR), composition/truncation moves the tails (SD only), and the SD-IQR divergence measures the composition share. Apply that logic to the exit estimates: SD +0.84, IQR -0.20. By the paper's own framework, the information component of the exit effect is the IQR estimate — which is *wrong-signed* (-0.20, s.e. 0.63) — and essentially all of the (insignificant) SD movement is composition. Yet Section 7.1 dollarizes the SD point estimate as "$240 of additional pricing-error dispersion per transaction" and matches the model to it. You cannot introduce the robust measure as the information-clean instrument in Sections 3-4 and then quietly calibrate to the contaminated one in Section 7. Done consistently, the information point estimate is zero-to-negative, which makes the "real, positive, and second-order" characterization rest entirely on the model's prior, not the data.

**What would change my mind:** Redo the magnitude discussion on the measure your own framework designates as the information channel (IQR-based), report its implied bound, and reconcile the wrong sign explicitly — or retract the SD-IQR decomposition claim and defend reading information off the SD.

### 3. Proposition 4(i) is quantitatively rejected by the paper's headline estimate, and the rescue is extra-model

The model's liquidity prediction is sharp: exit removes recorded volume one-for-one with the machine's *purchase* flow (Prop 4(i) — diverted movers return to the household market, so the net steady-state loss is purchases, not purchases-plus-resales). At mean exposure that is ~ 0.17 percent of volume. The estimate is -1.4 percent, and -1.7 percent excluding the wind-down: roughly **eight times** the model's prediction (the paper's "several times," benchmarked against the 0.3 percent two-deed footprint, understates the violation against its own Proposition). The paper then attributes the gap to a listing-margin/outside-option story plus the Ngai-Tenreyro thin-market multiplier — neither of which is in the model, which has exogenous listing (nu) and movers who always sell. This is the asymmetry that troubles me most as a structural reader: the model is credited for "predicting" the dispersion null in the channel where the test is unrejectable (Concern 1), while its one precisely-tested quantitative prediction fails by an order of magnitude and is excused in two sentences of borrowed intuition. The headline number of the paper is the one the theory does not explain.

**What would change my mind:** Either (a) extend the model with the margin the text already gestures at — non-movers' listing decision with the standing quote as the outside option in search — so the multiplier becomes a structural object whose magnitude is disciplined by an estimable quantity (the mass of marginal listings near indifference), and show plausible parameters generate ~8x; or (b) soften symmetrically: present the volume effect as reduced-form evidence of amplification *beyond* the model's mechanical channel, and stop describing the episode as one the model quantitatively organizes end-to-end.

### 4. Destruction versus retiming: the welfare claim outruns the design

The welfare section's first-order entry — "forgone transactions whose surplus simply did not occur" — requires that the lost trades were destroyed, not retimed or mechanically depleted. Two alternatives deserve confrontation. *Pull-forward/depletion:* exposure is, by construction, Zillow's 2018Q2-2021Q3 buying intensity; ZIPs where Zillow (over)bought heavily in the Project-Ketchup ramp had transactions pulled forward, and recently-traded houses do not re-trade for years — predicting post-2021 volume declines proportional to exposure with no externality at all. The ODshare placebo is your best defense (Opendoor bought *more* — mean 0.70 vs 0.17 — with gamma ~ 0), but it is confounded: Opendoor kept operating post-2021, mechanically supporting volume in its ZIPs. *Retiming:* if the quote's removal delayed rather than destroyed listings, the event-study coefficients should decay; you have data through 2024Q2 but the text describes the path only through 2022.

**What would change my mind:** The full event-study path through 2024 with an explicit decay test; cumulative volume accounting (is the gap permanent?); and ideally the RedfinNow November 2022 exit as a second, smaller event — your model and your multiplier story both predict a (scaled) replication, and depletion does not, since RedfinNow's 2021 ramp was modest.

### 5. Contribution positioning: what carries a QJE paper when the titular question returns an unrejectable bound?

The separation from Buchak et al. (2025) — the dealer's P&L versus the market's externality — is conceptually sharp and well-written, and the exit design (which-machine-was-your-ZIP-exposed-to, within metro-quarter) is the strongest asset here; it is teachable. But as the paper stands, the title, abstract framing, and model architecture all point at price discovery, and price discovery delivers a null whose "informativeness" depends on Concern 1 being resolved in your favor. The identified, novel result is the liquidity multiplier — which Proposition 4 does not explain. Meanwhile Corollary 1's selective-exit-by-forecast-quality claim — the structural reading of *why* the experiment exists — rests on a single self-published accuracy statistic (Zillow's 6.9 percent) with no comparable cross-firm number, while the paper's own institutional narrative (Project Ketchup overriding the algorithm) suggests the realized failure was as much governance as forecast variance. The pieces are individually defensible; the package currently asks the reader to accept the model where it cannot be tested, forgive it where it fails, and take the firm-heterogeneity mechanism on one firm's marketing statistic.

**What would change my mind:** A revision in which (i) the bound is made informative per Concern 1 or the framing is honestly inverted (a liquidity paper with a bounded information externality — which is what the abstract's last sentence already says: "what the market lost was a balance sheet, not a signal"); (ii) the multiplier gets a model home per Concern 3; and (iii) Corollary 1 is either supported with cross-firm forecast-error evidence (e.g., firm-level purchase-residual dispersion in your own deeds — you can compute Opendoor's and Zillow's sigma_a from the same hedonic) or presented as one of two observationally similar exit mechanisms.

## Minor concerns

- **Volume outcome definition is ambiguous.** Section 4.3 says outcomes are built from non-iBuyer trades "unless stated otherwise," but volume is "the count of arm's-length transactions in the cell" and is benchmarked against the machine's own footprint, implying machine deeds are included. State it explicitly — the mapping to Proposition 4(i) flips sign if volume is household-only (the model then predicts volume *rises* on exit as movers return).
- **Footnote 1 (endogenous sigma_xi)** asserts the fixed-point amplifies the comparative statics "without changing any sign." Prove it in the appendix or soften to a conjecture.
- **De-spreading is doing quiet work.** Third parties must recognize machine deeds and know the segment-specific m* = w + c (the purchase-discount component is not posted). Note that machine *resale* deeds are clean comps requiring no de-spreading — which implies the information channel partially survived through the 2022Q3 liquidation, a timing implication for the dispersion event study worth one sentence.
- **The "on top of" comp-window assumption** (machine deed adds to, never displaces, the k household comps) is generous to rho; report the displacement variant.
- **Heterogeneity (Table 4):** non-monotone tercile pattern, honestly flagged as underpowered — but the text should not lean on Figure 2's sorting as if it corroborates the *spillover* heterogeneity prediction; those are different objects.
- The phrase "the information externality is not rejected; it is sized" (Section 6.2) should go; per Concern 1, it is bounded, not sized.
- Verify the `calderwang2026algorithmic` reference is the published version; and QJE convention is three-decimal point estimates (Table 3 mixes precisions with the text's one-decimal log points).

## Positive observations

Three things deserve explicit credit. First, the paper's honesty discipline is the best I have seen in this literature: the entry design's failed pre-trends are disclosed and the design demoted to descriptive "without causal varnish"; the mechanical exposure-ramp problem in the exit event study is volunteered in the main text rather than extracted by referees; and Section 2.4 puts the machine's true (small) size on the table before any estimate appears. I would go further and consolidate these into a single labeled "what this paper does not show" paragraph — the raw material is all there. Second, Lemma 1's difference-of-squares profit identity is genuinely elegant, and the winner's-curse covariance (eq. 12) mapped line-by-line onto Zillow's Q3 2021 (9,680 bought, 3,032 sold, write-down language as accounting-speak for the curse) is economics writing of real quality. Third, the exit design itself — identifying off *which* machine a ZIP was exposed to, with the surviving machines absorbing both selection and the 2022 rate shock — is the kind of design QJE exists to publish.

**Recommendation: Major revision.**

The data infrastructure, the natural experiment, and the writing are QJE-caliber, and the paper's candor makes me trust the author to execute a serious revision. But the interpretive spine — "the model predicts both magnitudes" — does not currently hold: the dispersion calibration is unfalsifiable as built and internally inconsistent with the paper's own institutional numbers (Concerns 1-2), and the model's one testable magnitude fails by ~8x with the rescue grafted from outside (Concern 3). All five major concerns are addressable with data and derivations the author already has. A revision that disciplines the calibration against observable parameters, applies symmetric standards to the model's successes and failures, gives the liquidity multiplier either a structural home or a softer claim, and resolves destruction-versus-retiming would be a strong paper — quite possibly a different and better paper than the one the title currently promises.
