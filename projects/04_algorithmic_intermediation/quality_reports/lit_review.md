## Strand 1. iBuyers and algorithmic intermediation in housing

The anchor paper is **Buchak, Matvos, Piskorski & Seru, "Why Is Intermediating Houses So Difficult? Evidence from iBuyers," NBER Working Paper 28252 (December 2020, revised June 2025)** — first draft November 2019, previously circulated as *"iBuyers: Liquidity in Real Estate Markets?"* (2020 draft; PDF metadata and hosting pages confirm the same Buchak–Matvos–Piskorski–Seru team). Author websites report it as under revision at the *Journal of Political Economy* (R&R/conditional acceptance reported; **not yet verified as published — cite as NBER WP 28252**). Exact findings from the June 2025 revision:

- In Phoenix, the iBuyer transaction share grew from **about 1% at 2014 entry to around 6% by 2018**.
- iBuyers purchase at a **3.1 percentage-point discount** relative to similar listed homes, hold briefly (**roughly half of inventory resold within three months**), and earn an **average gross return ("spread") of about 5%**.
- Algorithmic pricing: observable characteristics plus ZIP-quarter fixed effects explain **over 80%** of price variation in iBuyer transactions versus **only 68%** in other transactions — i.e., iBuyers transact on algorithmizable information and are at a disadvantage on soft, hard-to-codify attributes (the "bad house odor" problem), generating **adverse selection** (Zillow reportedly had only ~10% of its offers accepted).
- iBuyers mitigate adverse selection by **selecting liquid, easy-to-value segments**: presence is lowest where algorithmic pricing errors are highest, profits fall as they expand into high-error segments, and they avoid houses with **less than a 50% probability of selling within three months**.
- A calibrated search model with a dealer facing adverse selection implies **over half of acquired homes are lower-quality**; removing the algorithm's residual ability to discriminate quality would shrink iBuyer market share **from ~5% to just above 1%**; allowing rental of inventory could raise share by **more than 7.5 percentage points**. Central trade-off: speed requires sacrificing valuation precision, which invites adverse selection — so machine intermediation works "precisely when additional liquidity is least needed."

Two published reduced-form papers study iBuyer *entry effects*. **Harrison, Seiler & Yang (2024), "The Impact of iBuyers on Housing Market Dynamics," Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics 68(3): 425–461** finds iBuyers enter neighborhoods with homogeneous, easily-priced homes; entry raises local house prices **by up to 2.8%**, driven partly by seller "fishing" (higher list prices, longer time-on-market); iBuyers crowd out local buyers into adjacent ZIP codes (raising prices there too) and increase transaction volume and off-market sales. **Seiler & Yang (2023), "The Burgeoning Role of iBuyers in the Housing Market," Real Estate Economics 51(3): 721–753** documents the same entry-selection on homogeneity and shows iBuyer acquisition discounts reflect compensation for liquidity/convenience to motivated sellers, not distressed purchases or unobserved quality.

**GAP (strand 1).** BMPS ask *whether intermediation is profitable for the dealer* and what frictions cap its scale; Harrison–Seiler–Yang and Seiler–Yang estimate *level effects of entry on prices and volume*. Nobody asks the market-quality question that defines the empirical microstructure literature: do algorithmic quotes make *prices more informative* for everyone else — faster mean reversion of idiosyncratic mispricing, lower within-segment price dispersion, smaller appraisal/list-price errors, less momentum? And no paper exploits the **abrupt November 2021 Zillow Offers shutdown** as a removal-of-the-machine natural experiment (two independent searches found no published or working paper doing so). Our design — staggered CBSA entry plus the Zillow exit DiD with Opendoor-exposed ZIPs as controls — measures the *externality* of algorithmic quotes on price discovery, not the dealer's P&L. That is the niche.

## Strand 2. Housing search, liquidity, and market microstructure

*Search-theoretic foundations.* **Wheaton (1990), "Vacancy, Search, and Prices in a Housing Market Matching Model," Journal of Political Economy 98(6): 1270–1292** introduced the matching framework where vacancy and search frictions determine prices. **Krainer (2001), "A Theory of Liquidity in Residential Real Estate Markets," Journal of Urban Economics 49(1): 32–53** ties time-on-market and prices to aggregate conditions ("hot and cold" markets). **Ngai & Tenreyro (2014), "Hot and Cold Seasons in the Housing Market," American Economic Review 104(12): 3991–4026** show thick-market effects amplify seasonal swings in prices and volume. **Genesove & Han (2012), "Search and Matching in the Housing Market," Journal of Urban Economics 72(1): 31–45** provide direct survey evidence on buyer search behavior and market tightness. **Piazzesi, Schneider & Stroebel (2020), "Segmented Housing Search," American Economic Review 110(3): 720–759** (note: AER, *not* JPE) document segment-level search clienteles in the Bay Area, with inventory and search activity comoving differently within versus across cities — directly relevant because iBuyer quote spillovers should operate within search segments. **Han & Strange (2015), "The Microstructure of Housing Markets: Search, Bargaining, and Brokerage," in Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics, Vol. 5, Elsevier, 813–886** is the survey our title speaks to.

*Price informativeness and predictability.* **Case & Shiller (1989), "The Efficiency of the Market for Single-Family Homes," American Economic Review 79(1): 125–137** established serial correlation in house-price changes — the original evidence that housing prices aggregate information slowly. **Guren (2018), "House Price Momentum and Strategic Complementarity," Journal of Political Economy 126(3): 1172–1218** rationalizes momentum through concave demand that makes sellers anchor on comparables; an algorithmic quote is precisely a new public anchor, so Guren's mechanism predicts iBuyer quotes can change momentum. **Glaeser & Nathanson (2017), "An Extrapolative Model of House Price Dynamics," Journal of Financial Economics 126(1): 147–170** model buyers who treat past prices as direct value signals, generating excess volatility. **Anenberg (2016), "Information Frictions and Housing Market Dynamics," International Economic Review 57(4): 1449–1479** estimates that sellers learn about demand from their own listing experience — a microfoundation for why public algorithmic signals should compress learning time.

*Dispersion, idiosyncratic risk, and liquidity measurement.* **Giacoletti (2021), "Idiosyncratic Risk in Housing Markets," Review of Financial Studies 34(8): 3695–3741** quantifies large idiosyncratic house-level return risk — the object an information-aggregating intermediary should shrink. **Jiang, Kotova & Zhang, "Liquidity in Residential Real Estate Markets" [WP, unverified journal status; SSRN 3386353, draft March 2024]** (the paper the task referenced as "Kotova & Zhang"; Erica Xuewei Jiang is a coauthor on the current draft) treat time-on-market and price dispersion as the quantity and price of liquidity in a supply–demand system — our headline outcomes (dispersion, TOM) come from this measurement framework.

*Asymmetric information in housing.* **Kurlat & Stroebel (2015), "Testing for Information Asymmetries in Real Estate Markets," Review of Financial Studies 28(8): 2429–2461** show better-informed sellers predict neighborhood price declines; **Stroebel (2016), "Asymmetric Information about Collateral Values," Journal of Finance 71(3): 1071–1112** shows informed lenders earn rents on soft information. Together they establish that dispersed private information is quantitatively important in housing — the precondition for an information-aggregation role for machine quotes.

**GAP (strand 2).** This literature documents slow information diffusion (Case–Shiller; Guren; Anenberg) and dispersed private information (Kurlat–Stroebel; Stroebel), but the arrival of a *public, firm, algorithmic quote* is a new kind of signal this literature has never had quasi-experimental variation in. We supply that variation in both directions (entry and abrupt exit).

## Strand 3. Information aggregation and market-maker theory

The theoretical scaffolding is canonical. **Akerlof (1970), "The Market for 'Lemons'," Quarterly Journal of Economics 84(3): 488–500** — adverse selection bounds on intermediation (the binding constraint in BMPS and in our model's "adverse-selection bound on machine intermediation"). **Grossman & Stiglitz (1980), "On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets," American Economic Review 70(3): 393–408** — prices cannot fully reveal costly information; an AVM that free-rides on past transaction prices inherits exactly this limit (and the Zillow failure is a clean illustration). **Glosten & Milgrom (1985), "Bid, Ask and Transaction Prices in a Specialist Market with Heterogeneously Informed Traders," Journal of Financial Economics 14(1): 71–100** and **Kyle (1985), "Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading," Econometrica 53(6): 1315–1335** — market-maker quotes impound information and define price discovery; the iBuyer is a Glosten–Milgrom dealer quoting against better-informed homeowners. **Duffie, Gârleanu & Pedersen (2005), "Over-the-Counter Markets," Econometrica 73(6): 1815–1847** and **Weill (2007), "Leaning Against the Wind," Review of Economic Studies 74(4): 1329–1354** — dealers providing liquidity in decentralized search markets, the right asset-market analogue for housing.

On algorithmic trading and price discovery in centralized markets: **Hendershott, Jones & Menkveld (2011), "Does Algorithmic Trading Improve Liquidity?" Journal of Finance 66(1): 1–33** (AT narrows spreads and makes quotes more informative); **Brogaard, Hendershott & Riordan (2014), "High-Frequency Trading and Price Discovery," Review of Financial Studies 27(8): 2267–2306** (HFT trades in the direction of permanent price changes, enhancing price discovery); **Biais, Foucault & Moinas (2015), "Equilibrium Fast Trading," Journal of Financial Economics 116(2): 292–313** (fast trading improves matching but generates adverse selection externalities — equilibrium investment in speed can be socially excessive).

**GAP (strand 3).** The equities evidence shows algorithmic liquidity provision improves price discovery in *centralized limit-order markets*. Whether the result transports to a *decentralized, opaque search market with heterogeneous assets* is an open question — theory cuts both ways (public quotes are informative signals à la Glosten–Milgrom, but Grossman–Stiglitz free-riding and Akerlof selection can make machine quotes noisy or even distortive). We provide the first quasi-experimental answer, including the sign of the effect when the machine *leaves*.

## Strand 4. Algorithms and AI in markets and real estate

**Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello (2020), "Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion," American Economic Review 110(10): 3267–3297** — Q-learning pricing algorithms autonomously learn supracompetitive prices. **Assad, Clark, Ershov & Xu (2024), "Algorithmic Pricing and Competition: Empirical Evidence from the German Retail Gasoline Market," Journal of Political Economy 132(3): 723–771** — margins rise ~28% in duopolies when both stations adopt algorithmic pricing. **Calder-Wang & Kim, "Algorithmic Pricing in Multifamily Rentals: Efficiency Gains or Price Coordination?" [WP, unverified journal status; SSRN 4403058, version February 2026]** — same-software adoption supports joint-profit-maximizing rents, ~$25/unit/month markup over ~4.2 million units, against measurable responsiveness (efficiency) gains. **Fuster, Goldsmith-Pinkham, Ramadorai & Walther (2022), "Predictably Unequal? The Effects of Machine Learning on Credit Markets," Journal of Finance 77(1): 5–47** — ML prediction technology redistributes surplus across borrower groups.

On AVMs/Zestimate specifically: **Fu, Huang, Mehta, Singh & Srinivasan (2025), "Unequal Impact of Zestimate on the Housing Market," Marketing Science 44(6): 1407–1427** — a structural model in which the Zestimate is a public signal that reduces buyer–seller uncertainty; both rich and poor neighborhoods gain, poor neighborhoods gain more (greater prior uncertainty), and equalizing algorithm accuracy across neighborhoods would raise total surplus by a further ~31%. **Fu, Jin & Liu, "Does Human-Algorithm Feedback Loop Lead to Error Propagation? Evidence from Zillow's Zestimate" [WP, unverified journal status; NBER WP 29880, 2022]** — the AVM updates on user-influenced inputs, creating feedback-loop error propagation. **Yu, "Algorithmic Outputs as Information Source: The Effects of Zestimates on Home Prices and Racial Bias in the Housing Market" [WP, unverified journal status; SSRN 3584896]** — exogenous Zestimate shocks move sale prices (Greater Philadelphia, 120k+ properties), i.e., market participants treat the algorithm's output as information. On AI and local/urban economies: **Alvarez, Argente, Chow & Van Patten, "Data Centers and Local Economies in the Age of AI: A Shift–Share Approach" [WP; NBER WP 35194]** — IV estimates show data-center expansion raises county employment, house prices, and electricity prices (note: Joyce Chow is a fourth coauthor beyond the trio in our notes).

**GAP (strand 4).** The Zestimate papers study a *non-tradable advisory signal*: Zillow publishes a number but bears no position. An iBuyer quote is a *firm, executable* price backed by the algorithm-owner's own capital — in microstructure terms, the difference between a newsletter and a dealer's bid. The algorithmic-pricing IO literature (Calvano; Assad et al.; Calder-Wang–Kim) studies sellers' pricing of their *own* inventory and competition effects. Our paper is the first to study an algorithm that *commits capital against its forecast* and ask whether that commitment disciplines or distorts price discovery for third parties — with the Zillow exit revealing what happens when the capital (but not the advisory signal, which persisted) is withdrawn. The Zillow-exit design also isolates the *quote* channel from the *information* channel, since the Zestimate stayed online after November 2021.

## Strand 5. Staggered-DiD econometrics

Standard modern toolkit, all verified: **Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021), "Difference-in-Differences with Multiple Time Periods," Journal of Econometrics 225(2): 200–230**; **Sun & Abraham (2021), "Estimating Dynamic Treatment Effects in Event Studies with Heterogeneous Treatment Effects," Journal of Econometrics 225(2): 175–199**; **Goodman-Bacon (2021), "Difference-in-Differences with Variation in Treatment Timing," Journal of Econometrics 225(2): 254–277**; **de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille (2020), "Two-Way Fixed Effects Estimators with Heterogeneous Treatment Effects," American Economic Review 110(9): 2964–2996**; **Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024), "Revisiting Event-Study Designs: Robust and Efficient Estimation," Review of Economic Studies 91(6): 3253–3285** (DOI 10.1093/restud/rdae007). For dose-response exposure to iBuyer activity: **Callaway, Goodman-Bacon & Sant'Anna, "Difference-in-Differences with a Continuous Treatment" [WP, unverified journal status; NBER WP 32117, 2024; latest arXiv version December 2025]** — TWFE with continuous doses requires stronger-than-parallel-trends assumptions to compare effects across doses; their `contdid` R package implements the estimators. Our staggered CBSA entry uses Callaway–Sant'Anna/Borusyak et al.; the ZIP-level Zillow-exposure design (share of 2019–21 transactions that were Zillow Offers purchases) is a continuous-treatment DiD and should cite CGBS for the selection-bias caveat on dose comparisons.

## Positioning summary (for the introduction)

1. **From dealer profitability to market quality.** BMPS (NBER 28252) establish *why machine intermediation is hard* (speed–accuracy trade-off, adverse selection, 3.1pp discount/5% spread, selection into easy-to-value homes); Harrison–Seiler–Yang (JREFE 2024) and Seiler–Yang (REE 2023) estimate *price-level and volume effects of entry*. We ask the Hendershott–Jones–Menkveld/Brogaard–Hendershott–Riordan question — does the algorithm improve *price discovery* (informativeness, dispersion, momentum, speed of idiosyncratic mean reversion) — in a decentralized market, where the equities answer need not carry over.
2. **A firm quote is not a Zestimate.** Relative to the AVM-as-public-signal literature (Fu et al. 2025 Mkt Sci; Yu WP), the iBuyer quote is executable and capital-backed; relative to algorithmic-pricing IO (Calvano 2020 AER; Assad et al. 2024 JPE), the algorithm here is a *market maker*, not a seller of its own goods. Glosten–Milgrom/Kyle logic plus the Akerlof/Grossman–Stiglitz limits give the theory a sharp, testable adverse-selection bound.
3. **The exit experiment is unclaimed.** Two systematic searches (June 2026) found no paper using the abrupt November 2021 Zillow Offers shutdown as a natural experiment for price discovery (it appears only as anecdote — e.g., BMPS footnote 3 and the management/AI-failure case literature). The shutdown removes the *firm quote* while leaving the *advisory signal* (Zestimate) in place — a uniquely clean separation of the two channels, with Opendoor-exposed ZIPs as comparison.
