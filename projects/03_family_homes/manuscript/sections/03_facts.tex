\section{Within-Family Housing Transfers: National Facts}\label{sec:facts}

\paragraph{Fact 1: For every 100 market sales, 17 family-transfer deeds are recorded.}
Table~\ref{tab:volumes} reports annual volumes and Figure~\ref{fig:volumes}
shows the pooled scale and annual path of the family channel.
Between 0.65 and 1.05 million family-transfer deeds are recorded every year
(same-surname and estate/executor), against 3.4 to 6.4 million market
sales: a family:market ratio between 0.14 and 0.22 in every year of the
sample, with a 2007--2023 aggregate of 0.17, i.e. 17 family-transfer deeds per 100 market sales.
% The conservative same-surname
% series alone runs 0.65 to 1.05 million per year i.e. 14 to 22 family-transfer deeds per 100 market sales.
The channel is not a recession artifact. The ratio stays between 0.20 and
0.22 from 2007 to 2012, while market sales were depressed after the
2008--2009 recession, and then declines to 0.14 by 2022--2023. Most of that decline is definitional: the
share of interfamily deeds without a surname match, which the main
definition excludes, rises from 17 percent in 2007 to 38 percent in 2023.
Adding those 6.1 million deeds back gives 19.7 million family deeds, 25 per
100 market sales, and a ratio that ends where it starts (0.24 in 2007, 0.23
in 2023). The main count is therefore conservative and, in practice, a lower
bound. At most 6.6 percent of same-surname deeds are coded arm's-length
(4.4 percent at \$100,000 or more), which bounds coincidental surname
matches; that is small next to the 6.1 million excluded deeds, many of which
are likely transfers to kin with a different surname, such as married
daughters. The count also includes spouses, who are family but not the
parent-child transfers Proposition~19 targets. It excludes the 1.2 to 1.6
million trust-mediated family transfers (1.5 to 2.1 per 100 market sales),
which more than double over the sample (from 43,000 in 2007 to 98,000 in
2023, lower bound); adding them raises the aggregate ratio to 0.19. It also
excludes 10.5 million trust deeds that are self-transfers or have no surname
link, 7.1 million same-person retitles, and 26.6 million other non-market
conveyances.\footnote{Restricting the denominator to
existing homes (excluding new-construction first sales) raises the aggregate
family:market ratio to 0.18. On a per-property rather than per-deed basis,
the 13.6 million family-transfer deeds cover 11.8 million distinct parcels
(1.15 deeds per parcel).}

\paragraph{Fact 2: Family transfers are concentrated in California.}
Figure~\ref{fig:stateratio} ranks states by the family:market ratio over
2007--2023. California leads the nation at 0.36---36 family transfers for
every 100 market sales, 2.1 times the pooled national ratio of 0.17. Utah is
second at 0.29. California is exactly where acquisition-value
assessment made the inherited tax basis most valuable, and it also accounts
for 39 percent of all interfamily trust deeds in the country (4.8 million over
the sample period, nearly twice its family transfers), consistent with extensive
estate-planning activity.

\paragraph{Fact 3: Family transfer shares are higher across middle-aged housing vintages.}
Linking deed events to property characteristics (60 percent of 2017--2023
selected family/trust/market events match the available property-characteristics snapshot; Appendix~\ref{app:data}),
the family share of within-class traffic rises with structure age: family
transfers are 13 percent of combined family-plus-market events for houses
of ten to thirty years, 14 percent at thirty to fifty years, and 16 percent
at fifty to seventy-five years, before falling to 14 percent for homes aged 75 or more. The gradient is therefore not monotone over all vintages; the under-ten-year bin also has very few matched events. The family share is lower on parcels with
senior tax exemptions (13 versus 15 percent)---an association measured with a historical snapshot, and one that reverses sign when market sales must carry a recorded price of at least \$10,000. Across the within-state value
distribution the family share is roughly flat at 15 percent through the
bottom four quintiles and falls to 13 percent in the top. The houses that
families keep lean toward middle-aged stock and away from the most expensive
homes. That is the stock through which housing filters down to lower-income buyers
\citep{rosenthal2014filtering}.

\paragraph{Family deeds are less common in condominiums and townhouses.}
Using recorded land-use codes, family deeds account for 16.0 percent of
combined family-plus-market deeds for single-family properties, 15.8
percent for duplexes, 9.1 percent for condominiums, and 8.5 percent
for townhouses or rowhouses. On common state-year support, weighting each
type to the single-family distribution leaves the duplex share 2.2
percentage points lower and the condominium and townhouse shares 7.6 and
6.4 points lower, respectively. Appendix~\ref{app:propertytypes} gives the
definitions and denominators. These are static recorded types, not measures
of occupancy or rental use; the apartment sample is selectively covered.
Duplexes, a common form of small income property, are not transferred
within families more often than single-family homes. We do not treat
building type as evidence that families pass on housing capital rather than
a residence.

\paragraph{Fact 4: Three in five family-transferred homes are still unsold a decade later.}
What happens to a house after it enters the family channel? Because the
parcel identifier links successive deeds, every transfer event can be
followed to the property's next arm's-length market sale, if any. We take all
events in the 2008--2018 cohorts---guaranteeing at least 66 months of
follow-up before the June 2024 censoring date---and estimate Kaplan--Meier
survival to first market sale, separately by event class:
8.5 million same-surname family transfers (plus 665 estate/executor deeds),
7.1 million interfamily trust deeds, 4.1 million same-person
retitles, and 46.5 million market purchases as the benchmark.

Table~\ref{tab:hazard} and Figure~\ref{fig:km} show that family homes lock
in. Same-surname transfers start slightly faster than market purchases (7.5
versus 6.7 percent sold within twelve months, \FamilySoldTwo\ versus \MarketSoldTwo{}
percent within two years) but fall behind by year five (\FamilySoldFive\ versus
\MarketSoldFive{} percent) and keep falling behind: by year ten, \FamilySoldTen\ percent of
same-surname family homes have returned to the market against \MarketSoldTen\ percent of
market purchases. The survival curves cross at about month 44, and the
five-year family sale rate is below the market rate in each of the eleven
annual cohorts from 2008 to 2018.

Conditional on surviving the first two years, \FamilyConditionalSold\
percent of family homes sell over the following eight years versus \MarketConditionalSold\
percent of market purchases. The market-purchase benchmark includes
investors and flippers. Without a price floor it also includes 4.5 million
deeds recorded with no price or a price below \$10,000, which resell within
twelve months more than three times as often as priced sales and so steepen
the early market curve. Three in
five (\FamilyRetainedTen\ percent) of the same-surname homes that passed between relatives in 2008--2018 had not sold
on the open market a decade later. An internal descriptive check is that
same-person retitles---deeds retaining at least one matched name---track interfamily trust deeds closely (9.7 versus
9.4 percent sold within two years), consistent with both classes often
recording paperwork rather than a change in the property's use.

This is a descriptive fact, and selection is its content: families keep the
homes they receive. Four caveats discipline its reading. First, the
comparison pools states and cohorts as family transfers concentrate in older
homes and in low-turnover states, so part of the gap is composition. Second, the two clocks
measure different objects: a market purchase starts a fresh tenure, while a
family deed often changes title without changing occupancy---a force that
should, if anything, \emph{raise} the family class's sale hazard, if
family deeds cluster near succession events. Third, some
family homes cannot sell for title reasons (heirs' property), a lock of a
different kind \citep{dobbsgaither2023heirs}. Fourth, interfamily trust deeds
---the trust-keyword class, mostly self-transfers---resell even more slowly
than same-surname family homes (21.9 versus 24.6 percent sold within five
years), a reminder that long-tenure households are exactly the
ones that do estate paperwork. These comparisons do not distinguish attachment, rental use, title constraints, and tax incentives. Proposition~19 identifies a response of deeds to a policy change; additional assumptions are needed to connect that response to allocation.
