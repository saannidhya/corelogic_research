\section{Within-Family Housing Transfers: National Facts}\label{sec:facts}

\paragraph{Fact 1: Family deeds account for \DeedNationalShare\ percent of all deed events.}
Table~\ref{tab:volumes} reports annual counts and Figure~\ref{fig:volumes} shows the pooled scale and annual path. In 2007--2023 the main family measure includes \DeedFamilyMillion\ million events. The numerator combines same-surname interfamily, estate/executor, and other interfamily deeds between two distinct people. The denominator includes all residential deed events in the sample: market sales, family deeds, trusts, retitles, and other conveyances. Annual shares range from \DeedShareMin\ to \DeedShareMax\ per 100 deed events. Adding back the deeds in which the same person or an entity is on both sides gives the upper bound of \DeedInclNationalShare\ per 100. These are shares of recorded events, not shares of distinct houses, households, or inheritances. Other counts are of the same order but measure something else. An industry tabulation puts inherited homes at about 7 percent of all US property transfers in the year to August 2025 and at 18 percent of California's in 2025 \citep{delventhal2026inherited}, and the Legislative Analyst's Office counts 60,000 to 80,000 inherited California properties a year that escape reassessment \citep{lao2017inheritance}. Those figures count inherited homes, or transfers that qualified for an exclusion, while ours count deeds between distinct people per deed event, including lifetime and spouse deeds and leaving out trust deeds, so the rates need not agree. The table also shows the trust-mediated candidates under the name rule and, from 2017, the chain-verified trust-to-heir deeds: about \SIIIHeirAvgNat\ a year, against about \SIIITrustToOtherAvgNat\ trust-to-person deeds under the name rule. The last column flags the 2018 break in affidavits of death with a trust party, which moves them out of the main family measure, mostly in California.

\paragraph{Fact 2: California has the highest family share of recorded deeds, and its lead is not mainly about parents and children.}
Figure~\ref{fig:stateratio} ranks the fifty states and the District of Columbia by pooled family deeds divided by all deed events over 2007--2023. California records \DeedCAShare\ per 100, \DeedCARelative\ times the national share. \SIIIRunnerUpState\ comes next, at \SIIIRunnerUpShare. The denominator includes California's extensive trust and retitling activity.

What is California's lead made of? Table~\ref{tab:decomp} splits the gap of \SIIIGapPerHundred\ deeds per 100 between California (\SIIICAPerHundred) and the other states (\SIIIRestPerHundred) by taxonomy cell. Cross-surname deeds account for \SIIIGapShareCrossSurname\ percent of the gap and parent-child candidates for \SIIIGapShareParentChild\ percent. By trigger, lifetime deeds are \SIIIGapShareLifetime\ percent and death-triggered deeds \SIIIGapShareDeath\ percent. The cross-surname cell is the weakest read in the taxonomy, so more than half of the lead sits where the data are least reliable. Spouse deeds, identified by document type, run \SIIISpousePerHundredCA\ per 100 deeds in California, equal to \SIIISpouseGapShare\ percent of the gap. But only California and Arizona identify them, so the spouse figure is not California's excess of spouse deeds, and other states' spouse deeds sit in other cells. Without the spouse deeds California alone identifies, its share is \SIIICAPerHundredNoSpouse\ against \SIIIRestPerHundred\ elsewhere. The California series crosses the 2018 coding break, which if anything lowers its later counts.

Can the data say why? We can test two correlates. Across the fifty states and the District of Columbia, the state family share without spouse deeds (2018--2023) has a correlation of \SIIICorrHPI\ with house-price growth since 2012 and \SIIICorrOld\ with residents aged 65 and over relative to the size of the deed market. The two together explain \SIIIStateRsq\ of the variation. Fitted without California, they predict \SIIICAPredShare\ per 100 for California, against \SIIICAShareNoSpouse\ in the data. Age and appreciation do not account for the lead. We have no state measure of the property-tax advantage, so the cross-section cannot say that the family channel rises with it. Section~\ref{sec:prop19} asks that question of one reform.

\paragraph{Fact 3: Family transfers vary across housing characteristics.}
Linking deeds to the available property-characteristics snapshot permits descriptive comparisons by vintage, value, and exemptions. Among matched family-plus-market events, the family share is \AgeShareYoung\ percent for structures aged ten to twenty-nine years, \AgeShareMiddle\ percent at thirty to forty-nine, and \AgeShareOlder\ percent at fifty to seventy-four. The corresponding shares are \SeniorShareYes\ percent with a recorded senior exemption and \SeniorShareNo\ percent without one. These profiles use a restricted family-plus-market denominator to preserve the existing correlates exercise; they are distinct from the all-deed shares in Figures~\ref{fig:volumes}--\ref{fig:stateratio}. Historical property characteristics do not establish occupancy or eligibility at the date of transfer.

Using recorded land-use codes, the family share of family-plus-market events is \TypeShareSF\ percent for single-family properties, \TypeShareDuplex\ for duplexes, \TypeShareCondo\ for condominiums, and \TypeShareTownhouse\ for townhouses. Appendix~\ref{app:propertytypes} reports type counts, common-support comparisons, and resale outcomes. Property type is a static recorded characteristic, not evidence that a particular family conveyance is a bequest or that its recipient occupies the property.

\paragraph{Fact 4: Family deeds are followed by a market sale sooner at first, yet more of them are unsold after ten years.}
We follow each 2008--2018 deed cohort to the next market sale or June 2024 censoring date. The main family cohort contains \MainCohortMillion\ million events, including \SurnameCohortMillion\ million same-surname and \OtherFamilyCohortMillion\ million other interfamily deeds between distinct people; the market benchmark contains \MarketCohortMillion\ million purchases. Table~\ref{tab:hazard} reports the pooled family outcome and its components separately.

Within twelve months, \MainSoldOne\ percent of main-family-measure deeds are followed by a market sale, versus \MarketSoldOne\ percent of market purchases. Within two years the shares are \FamilySoldTwo\ and \MarketSoldTwo\ percent. By five years they are \FamilySoldFive\ and \MarketSoldFive\ percent, and by ten years \FamilySoldTen\ and \MarketSoldTen\ percent. Thus \FamilyRetainedTen\ percent of main-family-measure events are not followed by a market sale within a decade, against \SIIIMarketUnsoldTen\ percent of market purchases. Conditional on remaining unsold for two years, \FamilyConditionalSold\ percent sell over the following eight years, compared with \MarketConditionalSold\ percent of market purchases.

Composition does not explain the early lead. Giving each market purchase the weight of the family deeds in its state and cohort year puts the market's resale shares at \SIIIMktRewSoldOne, \SIIIMktRewSoldTwo, \SIIIMktRewSoldThree\ and \SIIIMktRewSoldFive\ percent at twelve, twenty-four, thirty-six and sixty months (the added row of Table~\ref{tab:hazard}), below the family shares at every horizon. The reversal comes later, between five and ten years. These comparisons describe selected deeds. A family conveyance can change legal interests without changing occupancy, and attachment, title constraints such as heirs' property \citep{dobbsgaither2023heirs}, rental use and tax incentives can all shape the curves. Long retention is neither a measure of inefficient allocation nor an identified consequence of family ownership.
