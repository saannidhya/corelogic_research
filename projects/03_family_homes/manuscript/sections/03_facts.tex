\section{Within-Family Housing Transfers: National Facts}\label{sec:facts}

\paragraph{Fact 1: Family deeds account for \DeedNationalShare\ percent of all transfers.}
Table~\ref{tab:volumes} reports annual counts and Figure~\ref{fig:volumes} shows the pooled scale and annual path. In 2007--2023 the main definition includes \DeedFamilyMillion\ million events. The numerator combines same-surname interfamily, estate/executor, and other interfamily deeds. The denominator includes all residential deed events in the sample: market sales, family deeds, trusts, retitles, and other conveyances. Annual shares range from \DeedShareMin\ to \DeedShareMax\ per 100 deeds. These are shares of recorded events, not shares of distinct houses, households, or inheritances.

Panel A separates the surname-linked-plus-estate component (\DeedNarrowShare\ per 100 deeds) from other interfamily conveyances. The latter can include relatives with different surnames, but its relationship content is not independently verified. Panel B uses the complete main definition throughout. Trust deeds and same-person retitles are excluded from the family numerator and included in the all-deed denominator. Market sales retain the existing definition without a price floor.

\paragraph{Fact 2: California has the highest family share of recorded deeds.}
Figure~\ref{fig:stateratio} ranks the fifty states and the District of Columbia by pooled family deeds divided by all deed events over 2007--2023. California records \DeedCAShare\ per 100, \DeedCARelative\ times the national share; Utah follows at \DeedUTShare{}. The denominator includes California's extensive trust and retitling activity. It measures the composition of recorded deeds rather than ownership replacement or housing supply. Demography, legal institutions, name coverage, and recording practices can affect both numerator and denominator, so the cross-section does not identify the effect of inherited assessments.

\paragraph{Fact 3: Family transfers vary across housing characteristics.}
Linking deeds to the available property-characteristics snapshot permits descriptive comparisons by vintage, value, and exemptions. Among matched family-plus-market events, the family share is \AgeShareYoung\ percent for structures aged ten to twenty-nine years, \AgeShareMiddle\ percent at thirty to forty-nine, and \AgeShareOlder\ percent at fifty to seventy-four. The corresponding shares are \SeniorShareYes\ percent with a recorded senior exemption and \SeniorShareNo\ percent without one. These profiles use a restricted family-plus-market denominator to preserve the existing correlates exercise; they are distinct from the all-deed shares in Figures~\ref{fig:volumes}--\ref{fig:stateratio}. Historical property characteristics do not establish occupancy or eligibility at the date of transfer.

Using recorded land-use codes, the family share of family-plus-market events is \TypeShareSF\ percent for single-family properties, \TypeShareDuplex\ for duplexes, \TypeShareCondo\ for condominiums, and \TypeShareTownhouse\ for townhouses. Appendix~\ref{app:propertytypes} reports type counts, common-support comparisons, and resale outcomes. Property type is a static recorded characteristic, not evidence that a particular family conveyance is a bequest or that its recipient occupies the property.

\paragraph{Fact 4: Many family-transferred homes remain unsold for a decade.}
We follow each 2008--2018 deed cohort to the next market sale or June 2024 censoring date. The main family cohort contains \MainCohortMillion\ million events, including \SurnameCohortMillion\ million same-surname and \OtherFamilyCohortMillion\ million other interfamily deeds; the market benchmark contains \MarketCohortMillion\ million purchases. Table~\ref{tab:hazard} reports the pooled family outcome and its components separately.

Within twelve months, \MainSoldOne\ percent of main-definition family deeds are followed by a market sale, versus \MarketSoldOne\ percent of market purchases. Within two years the shares are \FamilySoldTwo\ and \MarketSoldTwo\ percent. By five years they are \FamilySoldFive\ and \MarketSoldFive\ percent, and by ten years \FamilySoldTen\ and \MarketSoldTen\ percent. Thus \FamilyRetainedTen\ percent of main-definition family events are not followed by a market sale within a decade. Conditional on remaining unsold for two years, \FamilyConditionalSold\ percent sell over the following eight years, compared with \MarketConditionalSold\ percent of market purchases. The other-interfamily component has its own early resale pattern (\OtherFamilySoldOne\ percent at twelve months and \OtherFamilySoldTwo\ percent at twenty-four months), which may include settlement-related conveyances as well as longer-term retention.

These comparisons describe selected deeds and properties. A family conveyance can change legal interests without changing occupancy; an inherited rental may continue serving the same tenant. State and cohort composition, attachment, title constraints such as heirs' property \citep{dobbsgaither2023heirs}, rental use, and tax incentives can all influence the curves. The market benchmark includes investors and unpriced arm's-length deeds. Long retention is therefore neither a measure of inefficient allocation nor an identified consequence of family ownership.
