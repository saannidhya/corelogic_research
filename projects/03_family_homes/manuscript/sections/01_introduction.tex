\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The housing market that economists study is the one with prices.
For half a century, that market has been one of the most productive objects
in applied economics. Hedonic methods recover what buyers pay for each
feature of a home \citep{rosen1974hedonic}, repeat-sales indexes track how
the value of the same house moves over time
\citep{baileymuthnourse1963regression,case1989efficiency}, and search models
explain why homes sit unsold, why prices and volume rise together, and why
sellers hold out for prices the market will not pay
\citep{wheaton1990vacancy,genesovemayer2001loss,genesove2012search,ngai2014hot,piazzesi2020segmented}.
The same transactions tell us how regulation limits new supply
\citep{glaesergyourkosaks2005manhattan,hsiehmoretti2019housing} and how
often owners move when prices fall or rates rise
\citep{ferreiragyourkotracy2010busts,fonsecaliu2024lockin}.
Housing turnover is measured through market sales. Sales are recorded, indexed and aggregated.
Each of these literatures starts, sensibly, from a sale between strangers,
because presumably only such a sale reveals what a home is worth. However, many homes change hands without a market. Families transfer homes to each other by gift, inheritance, and other non-market conveyances -- a father quitclaiming a bungalow to his son, or siblings dividing a family estate among themselves. These deed transfers are recorded, but they usually do not generate a market price, and thereby ignored by the literature as ``non-arms length'' transactions.
For anyone building a price index, that filter is the right one. A deed from
a mother to her daughter for one dollar says little about what the house is
worth. But the filter also discards a question: who ends up living in a home
when no price is ever consulted? An older literature does study what passes
between generations, yet it focuses on dollars rather than houses. Economists have
long disagreed about how much of American wealth is inherited rather than
saved over a lifetime \citep{kotlikoffsummers1981role,modigliani1988role},
and about why parents give their wealth to their children: to help the child who earns less, to
reward the child who visits, or because they did not expect to die so soon
\citep{beckertomes1979equilibrium,bernheimshleifersummers1985bequest,mcgarry1999intervivos,kopczuklupton2007leave,kopczuk2007bequest}.
Inherited wealth has regained weight in rich economies
\citep{piketty2011inheritance}, the home is the largest asset in the
portfolio of the typical middle-class American family
\citep{kuhnschularicksteins2020income}, and housing wealth persists strongly
across generations \citep{charleshurst2003wealth,binderrischvoorheis2026housing}.
Yet the bequest literature treats a house as a sum in an estate, while the
housing literature treats the same deed as noise to be cleaned out of a price
index. Neither follows the home itself. That omission matters because houses
are durable and outlive their owners \citep{glaesergyourko2005durable}, and
when prices do not allocate homes, nothing ensures that each home goes to the
person who values it most. Rent control misallocates apartments across
tenants \citep{glaeserluttmer2003rentcontrol}. Property taxes can do the
same thing to owners. Under an assessment cap, such as California's
Proposition~13, a home is taxed on its purchase price plus at most two
percent a year, so a long-time owner's tax bill falls far below what a new
buyer would pay for the same house. Selling forfeits that discount, because
moving even across the street means paying the market tax on the next home.
The low bill
is therefore a reward for staying put, and it should lock owners in, as
simulations predicted that it would
\citep{osullivansextonsheffrin1995property}. But the first comparison of
California's metropolitan areas with those of other states found no clear
change in mobility after 1978 \citep{nagy1997proposition}. With longer data,
the property tax lock-in became visible: California owners now stay in their homes longer
than owners in comparable states \citep{wasiwhite2005prop13}, and letting
older Californians carry their assessment to a new home made them more likely
to move \citep{ferreira2010takeit}. Florida's caps on assessment growth have
raised the same question \citep{ihlanfeldt2011caps}. The tax discount works much like a below-market
mortgage rate, which also keeps owners from moving
\citep{ferreiragyourkotracy2010busts,fonsecaliu2024lockin}. Recent work
shows how such taxes shape who holds which homes when buyers face financial
constraints \citep{covenetal2024proptax}. Transaction taxes, for their part,
change when and whether homes trade at all
\citep{kopczukmunroe2015mansion,hilberlyytikainen2017transfer,bestkleven2018notches}.
A family transfer can have both features at once. The home moves without a
price, and in California it could, until recently, carry the parent's tax
basis with it. Before Proposition~19, the inheritance exclusion let the
lock-in outlive the owner. An heir who kept the house kept the parent's tax
bill, sometimes decades old. An heir who sold it gave that discount up,
because the buyer would be taxed at market value. The older homes that families keep are also the homes that
would otherwise filter down to new and often poorer buyers
\citep{rosenthal2014filtering}. In this paper, we study one of the largest non-market channels of housing turnover: within-family transfers. We document their scale and persistence, and we study how they respond to property-tax incentives.
We find that this channel is not small. From 2007 through 2023, county recorders logged
13.6 million family-transfer deeds, about 17 for every 100 market sales.
The annual ratio was 0.20 to 0.22 through 2012 and fell to 0.14 by
2022--2023. Much of that fall reflects how we count. Our main definition
requires the buyer and seller of an interfamily deed to share a surname, and
the share of interfamily deeds without a surname match rose from 17 to 38
percent. Adding those deeds gives about 25 transfers per 100 market sales
(Table~\ref{tab:volumes}), and that wider ratio is roughly flat. The main
count is therefore conservative and, in practice, a lower bound: at most 6.6
percent of same-surname deeds are coded arm's-length, while the 6.1 million
excluded deeds without a surname match likely include many kin with different
surnames, such as married daughters.
Understanding this channel requires
measuring both its scale and the institutions that shape it.

This paper has three parts. First, it establishes national facts about
within-family transfers using 144 million residential deed transfer events.
We document that family deeds represent a substantial flow alongside market
sales in every year. Their ratio to market sales is highest in California
(36 per 100, about twice the national ratio), where the tax
code historically allowed heirs to inherit a property's assessment as well
as the property. Their share rises across middle-aged housing vintages,
although it is not monotone over all ages. Many properties remain unsold
long after a family transfer.

Second, the paper examines how tax incentives shape recorded family
transfers using California's Proposition~19. Under Proposition 13, Propositions~58 and 193, growth rate of assessment values of homes in California were capped at 2 percent per year, and eligible intergenerational transfers could preserve the parent's assessed
value, thereby allowing heirs to inherit a below-market property-tax assessment along with the house. Proposition~19 narrowed this exclusion in February 2021, retaining
conditional protection for a principal residence occupied by the heir and
for family farms while removing it for other qualifying transfers
\citep{boe2025prop19}. We use the ballot placement and implementation of Proposition~19 as a natural experiment to study how inherited property-tax assessments shape the family transfer channel.

We find that California family deeds ran 21.4 percent above a simple
counterfactual from the June 2020 ballot placement to the February 2021
deadline, and a monthly event study shows the largest seasonally adjusted
spike in the deadline month. That 21.4 percent overstates anticipation.
California deeds were already above the counterfactual in early 2020, before
the ballot placement, partly a rebound from an unusually low 2018--2019 base.
Family deeds then fell to a persistently
lower level. A triple difference that compares family deeds with market
sales, in California and in other states, before and after the reform, shows
family transfers \WelfareNetPercent\ percent lower in 2022--2023 net of California's
market-sale decline, roughly \WelfareFlow\ missing deeds annually when
scaled by the 2018--2019 baseline. A synthetic control and a monthly event
study trace the same persistent post-reform gap. 

Third, we develop a simple framework that quantifies the potential allocation gains from removing the inherited-assessment advantage. The framework follows a marginal heir's decision to keep a home or sell it, and the components involved in that decision: the value of the home to the heir, the market value of the home, and the inherited property-tax assessment. The framework shows that the tax advantage tips only those heirs who value the home less than the market does, and we measure the distortion using the tax wedge for each parcel. We estimate each component of the tax wedge using the 2018--2019 cohort of California family transfers, and we combine the estimated tax wedge with our framework's predictions to quantify the potential deadweight loss from the misallocation. The homes that California families passed down in 2018--2019 carried a median wedge of \$\WelfareWedgeMedian\ a year in 2023 dollars, including homes with zero wedges. Their total inherited tax savings were about \$\WedgeInheritedFlowM\ million a year, a transfer from other taxpayers to heirs rather than a loss to the economy. The deadweight loss is the much smaller triangle beneath that transfer: if each of the \WelfareFlow\ missing deeds a year sent a home to a higher-value user, assigning each home the median wedge and a distortion equal to half that wedge would yield about \$\WelfareDWLCohortMedian\ million a year of avoided deadweight loss per annual cohort.


The paper connects the economics of bequests and inter-vivos transfers
\citep{bernheimshleifersummers1985bequest,mcgarry1999intervivos,kopczuk2007bequest,piketty2011inheritance}
to an asset-level record of housing ownership. Research on intergenerational
wealth transmission \citep{charleshurst2003wealth} motivates the importance
of the asset, while evidence on heirs' property documents a distinct source
of title-related immobility \citep{dobbsgaither2023heirs,burnettwm2025heirs}.
Recent work studies intergenerational housing-capital persistence using
linked Census, property, and income-tax records
\citep{binderrischvoorheis2026housing}. Our deed-based object is the
conveyance of particular assets, rather than the persistence of children's
housing holdings. Closely related work by
\citet{bakerkimbiswas2026inherited} uses parent-child exclusion claims in
Los Angeles and San Francisco to study Proposition~19 retiming and
subsequent sales. The present national measurement and California-wide
comparisons complement that evidence. Our recorder-based family proxy is not
the population of confirmed parent-child exclusion claims: it includes
spouses, who are family but not the transfers Proposition~19 targets, and it
misses heirs with a different surname.
The nearest descriptive antecedents also include industry tabulations
\citep{delventhal2026inherited} and the LAO's assessment-roll accounting.

The analysis also extends the property-tax and housing lock-in literature
\citep{wasiwhite2005prop13,ferreira2010takeit,fonsecaliu2024lockin,covenetal2024proptax}
to intergenerational title transfers. The transaction-tax literature
\citep{kopczukmunroe2015mansion,slemrodwebershan2017dc,bestkleven2018notches,hilberlyytikainen2017transfer}
provides a comparison for timing and volume responses, although a deadline
response alone does not reveal the valuation gaps required for welfare.
Misallocation concerns, as in \citet{glaeserluttmer2003rentcontrol}, require
evidence or assumptions about forgone surplus. Older housing can serve an
important filtering role \citep{rosenthal2014filtering}, but a family-owned
rental may already provide those services to a tenant.
