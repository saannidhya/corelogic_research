\section{Family Channel: A Simple Framework}\label{sec:framework}\label{sec:welfare}

The policy experiment identifies a response of recorded deeds. Translating
that response into welfare requires two further steps: determining which
properties change allocation, and valuing those changes. This section makes
those steps explicit. Its quantitative results are conditional calibrations
of potential gains from the inheritance provisions, not estimates of
realized gains or the total welfare effect of Proposition~19.

\subsection{Framework}

% Consider a price-taking household under quasi-linear preferences, with
% fixed housing services and no externalities. 

Consider a price-taking marginal market buyer and a marginal heir. 
Let $u_m$ be the flow per-period value
of the house to the marginal market buyer and $u_h$ be the value of the house to the heir, 
including attachment and the net value of rental
use. 
% These are values of housing services, not recorded deed prices. 

Suppose the property-tax rate is $t$, assessments reset to the sale price when a property is sold, and the discount rate is $r$. Capitalizing the perpetual tax burden, the marginal market price is

\begin{equation}
  p=\frac{u_m}{r+t}.
\end{equation}


Now consider an heir at the moment of succession who values
living in the house, or renting it out, at flow $u_h$. If the family keeps the house, the heir
pays taxes on assessed value $A$. If the family sells, the heir receives $\$p$ and the house exits the family channel. Since the heir's inherited assessment is $A$, keeping the house is optimal when


\begin{equation}
  \frac{u_h-tA}{r}\geq p
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  u_h\geq u_m-t(p-A).
  \label{eq:succession_keep}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:succession_keep} contains the framework's key trade-off. If
succession triggers reassessment ($A=p$), the condition collapses to
$u_h\geq u_m$: the house stays in the family only when the heir genuinely
values it more than the market does, and the family channel is allocatively
harmless. But if the heir inherits the parent's assessment
($A=A_0<p$), the family keeps the house whenever
$u_h\geq u_m-t(p-A_0)$. Every heir with
$u_h\in[u_m-t(p-A_0),u_m)$ retains a house that the market values more than
she does. Figure~\ref{fig:retention_wedge} illustrates this interval.
The wedge $t(p-A_0)$, the annual tax saving from dynastic
holding, is the extra benefit the tax policy pays families to keep the houses in the family channel that would otherwise be sold in the housing market. 
% In California, where acquisition-value assessment dates to 1978 and house prices have multiplied since, the wedge on a long-held home is measured in thousands of dollars per year.

In our setup, the succession is a joint decision of the parent and the heir, and the recorded family transfer is the outcome of the keep-or-sell choice. When the family keeps, the
county records a family deed; when it cashes out, the family sells directly
to a market buyer and no family transfer ever appears\footnote{This simple framework abstracts from intermediary ownership structures and multiple deeds for the same property. An extension could allow a family to transfer the property to a trust before it reaches the market, or could model successive recorded deeds within a single allocation change.}. The heir's decision is therefore a function of the tax wedge, the market price, and her own valuation. Thus, making the deed transfer decision
endogenous to the tax regime. Introducing a policy reform that removes the inherited assessment ($A_0\to p$), the following three predictions follow from the framework.
First, retiming: because a family transfer must be completed before the deadline to preserve the tax advantage, the families rush to
lock
in $A_0$. Families with latent transfer plans and valuation within $[u_m-t(p-A_0),u_m)$ rush to record deeds before
the effective date, and recorded transfers collapse afterward
\citep[cf. the transaction-tax notch literature:][]{bestkleven2018notches,
kopczukmunroe2015mansion,slemrodwebershan2017dc}.
Second, the extensive margin: with the wedge removed, every heir still facing the succession decision and with valuations in
$[u_m-t(p-A_0),u_m)$ switches out of the recorded family-transfer channel.
The steady-state volume of
family transfers falls by the mass of heirs remaining in the wedge, and the reform potentially releases the homes to the market.
Third, selection: the family transfers that survive are those with
$u_h\geq u_m$---heirs who genuinely want the house, disproportionately
owner-occupants, whom the surviving exclusion 
still favors. The framework therefore predicts that post-reform family
transfers should show a lower subsequent market-sale hazard and a lower
absentee-recipient share---not because the reform failed, but because the
marginal would-have-been-landlord heirs no longer take the title.
The data do not confirm this third prediction. In
Table~\ref{tab:prop19}, the absentee-recipient share of California family
transfers does not fall (column 6: $+0.2$ percentage points), and their
24-month resale rate falls only slightly more than that of market purchases
(1.7 versus 1.1 percentage points, columns 4 and 5).

\subsection{Estimating the tax wedge}\label{subsec:wedge}

In this section, we estimate the tax wedge $w=t(p-A_0)$ for California's homes that inherit the parent's assessment explained in Section~\ref{sec:framework}. Since the wedge is not recorded in any deed, we build it parcel by parcel from three components: the tax rate $t$, the inherited assessment $A_0$, and the market value $p$. Each term is measured at a January~1 lien date, the date on which California fixes assessments. 
A deed transfer associated with market sale records a price, and family deeds usually record none. CoreLogic's assessment roll also stops in 2008--2009. We therefore build each of the
three terms parcel by parcel and estimate
\begin{equation}
  \hat w_i=\hat t_i\,\max\bigl(\hat p_i-\hat A_i,\,0\bigr)
  \label{eq:wedge_hat}
\end{equation}
for the \WedgeParcels\ single-family and condominium parcels on the
2008--2009 California roll. The main lien date is
January 2019, the year before Proposition~19 is introduced on the ballot. Further, January 2017 is used for homes inherited in 2018--2019, so that
their wedge is measured before Proposition~19 reached the ballot.\footnote{
The wedge is truncated at zero because a home whose market value has fallen
below its factored base is assessed at market value, and inheriting its
assessment saves nothing.} Next, we describe the three components of the wedge and their construction.

\paragraph{The tax rate, $\hat t$.} Proposition~13 caps the general levy at
one percent of assessed value, and voter-approved levy adds to this one-percent base. We divide
each parcel's total tax bill on the 2008--2009 roll by its assessed value\footnote{Our data provides the tax bill and the assessed value for 2008-2009 years. We use the latest year available for each parcel. A parcel with a missing or unusable ratio receives its county's median.}  We bound rates between 0.8 and 2.0 percent, which binds for
\WedgeRateLowerShare\ percent of parcels at the bottom and
\WedgeRateUpperShare\ percent at the top. The median rate is
\WedgeMedianRate\ percent. Because the bill can include fixed charges that do
not scale with value, this ratio can overstate the rate an heir would pay on a
higher assessment.

\paragraph{The inherited assessment, $\hat A$.} Proposition~13 sets a home's
base at its purchase price and lets it rise each year by the inflation
factor the State Board of Equalization publishes, never more than two
percent. The base resets only at a change in ownership. We start from the
2008--2009 assessed value and roll it forward with the published inflation factors or 2 percent, whichever is lower. If the parcel sold at arm's length after the roll date, the
base resets to that sale price and rolls forward from the sale year. Family
transfers do not reset the base, because Propositions~58 and 193 excluded
parent-child and grandparent-grandchild transfers from reassessment. At the
2019 lien date, \WedgeShareRollBase\ percent of parcels carry the rolled-forward
roll value and \WedgeShareSaleReset\ percent a base reset by a later sale.
% One caution applies to the roll path. Assessments enrolled in the 2008--2009 downturn may be temporary Proposition~8 reductions rather than the protected base; rolling such a value forward at two percent understates $A$ and overstates the wedge.

\paragraph{The market value, $\hat p$.} Since unsold houses have no price for the year 2019, we estimate a hedonic model to impute the predicted market value for each parcel. 
Our hedonic model is estimated on arm's-length, non-distressed
California sales from 2016--2017. It regresses the log price on the log of
floor area, lot size, bedroom and bathroom counts, and age, with ZIP-code by
property-type and sale-month fixed effects. We trim gross outliers, refit,
and correct the retransformation from logs to dollars with Duan's smearing
factor. Every parcel is then valued at the January 2017 price level and moved
to the lien date with a county repeat-sales index. The full model values
\WedgeAVMShareFull\ percent of parcels, and the rest use a simple coarser method as a fallback. On
\WedgeAVMHoldout\ randomly held-out sales, the model explains
\WedgeAVMRsq\ of the variance in log prices, its median absolute error is
\WedgeAVMMedianAPE\ percent, and \WedgeAVMWithinTwenty\ percent of predictions
fall within 20 percent of the recorded price.

\subsubsection{Tax wedge distribution and deadweight loss}

Figure~\ref{fig:wedge_distribution} shows the distribution of the
estimated wedge across California single-family and condominium parcels in 2019.
Most homes carry a positive wedge as only \WedgeShareZero\ percent have a zero wedge, mainly
homes bought after the 2012 trough whose base is still close to market
value. Among homes with a positive wedge, the median annual saving is
\$\WedgeMedianPos\ in 2019 dollars, as marked by the figure's reference line.
The wedge is a creature of tenure, exactly as Proposition~13 implies. Homes with no market sale since 2007 and a historical homeowner exemption carry a median positive wedge of \$\WedgeLongHeldHOXMedian.

An outside benchmark helps discipline these numbers. The Legislative
Analyst's Office put the annual saving on a long-held Los Angeles home at
\$3,000 to \$4,000 \citep{lao2017inheritance}. For the \WedgeLAOParcels\
Los Angeles County homes with no market sale since 2007, our median wedge in
2017 is \$\WedgeLAOMedian. The two populations differ, since our definition
of long-held is coarser, but the estimates are of the same size.

The homes that matter for welfare are the ones families actually pass
down. In 2018--2019, before the reform reached the ballot,
\WedgeInheritedPerYear\ parcels a year in our universe left their owners
through a family transfer (an interfamily deed between parties with the same
surname, or an estate deed).\footnote{This count includes transfers between
spouses, which keep their base under a separate exclusion that
Proposition~19 did not change. It misses heirs with a different surname and
distributions from trusts. The Legislative Analyst's Office estimated that 60,000 to
80,000 properties a year received the inheritance exclusion
\citep{lao2017inheritance}. The two counts measure different things; they
are of the same order of magnitude.} Measured at the January 2017 lien date, their
median wedge is \$\WedgeInheritedMedianNominal, or
\$\WedgeInheritedMedian\ in 2023 dollars, including homes with zero wedges.\footnote{
The inherited-home mean is \$\WedgeInheritedMeanNominal\ in 2017 dollars,
or \$\WedgeInheritedMean\ in 2023 dollars. It includes zero wedges and
refers to the 2018--2019 family-transfer sample. The \$\WedgeMeanPos\ mean
in Table~\ref{tab:wedge_components} instead conditions on positive wedges
across the January 2019 parcel stock and is expressed in 2019 dollars.}
Together these homes
carried about \$\WedgeInheritedFlowM\ million a year of inherited tax savings.
That sum is a transfer from taxpayers to heirs, not a loss to the economy.
Most of it goes to heirs who would have kept the home anyway.

The deadweight loss comes only from the heirs the subsidy changes. In the
framework, an heir with valuation $u_h\in[u_m-w,u_m)$ keeps a home the market
values more. Each such home loses $g=u_m-u_h$ per year, somewhere between zero
and $w$. If we make a simplifying assumption that valuations are spread evenly across that interval, the average loss
is the Harberger triangle
\begin{equation}
 E[g\mid\text{allocation switcher},w]
   =\frac{1}{w}\int_0^w g\,dg=\frac{w}{2}.
 \label{eq:triangle}
\end{equation}
More generally, write the average loss as $\lambda w$ with
$0\leq\lambda\leq1$; we report $\lambda=0.25,0.5,0.75$. Uniformity is an
assumption about a finite reform, not something deadline bunching reveals
\citep{chetty2009sufficient}.

For a median-based illustration, suppose every one of the \WelfareFlow\ deeds a year
that the triple difference finds missing is a home that now goes to a
higher-value user. Assigning each home the inherited-home median wedge
of \$\WelfareWedgeMedian\ and setting $\lambda=1/2$ gives
\$\WelfareDWLCohortMedian\ million a year of avoided deadweight loss for
each annual cohort, in 2023 dollars. This is a representative-home
calibration; aggregating heterogeneous wedges instead requires their mean.
Using the inherited-home mean gives \$\WelfareDWLCohort\ million.
Both calculations assume every missing deed reallocates a home. The reallocation share
of missing deeds, $\alpha$, is not identified: a missing deed may be a
postponed succession, a title held by a different vehicle, or a family that
kept the home for reasons the tax never touched.
