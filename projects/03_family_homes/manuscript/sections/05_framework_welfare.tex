\section{Welfare: The Tax Wedge and a Conditional Loss}\label{sec:framework}\label{sec:welfare}

Section~\ref{sec:prop19} is about deeds. A deed is not a house, and a missing deed is not a loss. Turning the response of deeds into a welfare number takes two steps. The first is to measure what an inherited assessment is worth to an heir each year, the wedge of Section~\ref{subsec:framework_v2}. The second is to decide how many of the missing transfers were homes that would otherwise have gone to someone who valued them more. This section takes the first step with data and the second with stated assumptions. Its results are conditional calibrations, not estimates of the effect of Proposition~19.

\subsection{Estimating the tax wedge}\label{subsec:wedge}

In this section, we estimate the tax wedge $w=t(p-A_0)$ for California's homes that inherit the parent's assessment, the wedge of the framework in Section~\ref{subsec:framework_v2}. Since the wedge is not recorded in any deed, we build it parcel by parcel from three components: the tax rate $t$, the inherited assessment $A_0$, and the market value $p$. Each term is measured at a January~1 lien date, the date on which California fixes assessments. 
Market sales record a price, and family deeds usually record none. CoreLogic's assessment roll also stops in 2008--2009. We therefore estimate each term and compute
\begin{equation}
  \hat w_i=\hat t_i\,\max\bigl(\hat p_i-\hat A_i,\,0\bigr)
  \label{eq:wedge_hat}
\end{equation}
for the \WedgeParcels\ single-family and condominium parcels on the
2008--2009 California roll. The main lien date is
January 2019, the year before Proposition~19 reached the ballot. For homes inherited in 2018--2019, January 2017 is used instead, so that
their wedge is measured before Proposition~19 reached the ballot.\footnote{
The wedge is truncated at zero because a home whose market value has fallen
below its factored base is assessed at market value, and inheriting its
assessment saves nothing.} Next, we describe the three components of the wedge and their construction.

\paragraph{The tax rate, $\hat t$.} Proposition~13 caps the general levy at
one percent of assessed value, and voter-approved levies add to this base. We divide
each parcel's total tax bill on the 2008--2009 roll by its assessed value\footnote{Our data provides the tax bill and the assessed value for the 2008--2009 roll years. We use the latest year available for each parcel. A parcel with a missing or unusable ratio receives its county's median.}  We bound rates between 0.8 and 2.0 percent, which binds for
\WedgeRateLowerShare\ percent of parcels at the bottom and
\WedgeRateUpperShare\ percent at the top. The median rate is
\WedgeMedianRate\ percent. Because the bill can include fixed charges that do
not scale with value, this ratio can overstate the rate an heir would pay on a
higher assessment.

\paragraph{The inherited assessment, $\hat A$.} Proposition~13 sets a home's
base at its purchase price and lets it rise each year by the inflation
factor the State Board of Equalization publishes, never more than two
percent. The base resets only at a change in ownership. We start from the
2008--2009 assessed value and roll it forward with the published inflation factors or 2 percent, whichever is lower. If the parcel sold at arm's length after the roll date, the
base resets to that sale price and rolls forward from the sale year.
For the potential inherited-assessment advantage, we preserve the rolled-forward
base across non-market deeds, conditional on eligibility for the former exclusion.
Propositions~58 and 193 protected qualifying parent-child and grandparent-grandchild
transfers; the broader deed proxy does not establish whether an exclusion was claimed.
At the
2019 lien date, \WedgeShareRollBase\ percent of parcels carry the rolled-forward
roll value and \WedgeShareSaleReset\ percent a base reset by a later sale.
% One caution applies to the roll path. Assessments enrolled in the 2008--2009 downturn may be temporary Proposition~8 reductions rather than the protected base; rolling such a value forward at two percent understates $A$ and overstates the wedge.

\paragraph{The market value, $\hat p$.} Since most homes have no sale in 2019, we estimate a hedonic model to impute the predicted market value for each parcel. 
Our hedonic model is estimated on arm's-length, non-distressed
California sales from 2016--2017. It regresses the log price on the log of
floor area, lot size, bedroom and bathroom counts, and age, with ZIP-code by
property-type and sale-month fixed effects. We trim gross outliers, refit,
and correct the retransformation from logs to dollars with Duan's smearing
factor. Every parcel is then valued at the January 2017 price level and moved
to the lien date with a county repeat-sales index. The full model values
\WedgeAVMShareFull\ percent of parcels, and the rest use a simple coarser method as a fallback. On
\WedgeAVMHoldout\ randomly held-out sales, the model explains
\WedgeAVMRsq\ of the variance in log prices, its median absolute error is
\WedgeAVMMedianAPE\ percent, and \WedgeAVMWithinTwenty\ percent of predictions
fall within 20 percent of the recorded price.

\subsection{The loss from misallocation}\label{subsec:loss}

The wedge is a transfer before it is a loss. An heir who keeps the home saves $w$ a year at other taxpayers' expense, and the heir who would have kept it anyway changes nothing but who pays. The loss comes only from heirs the tax tips: those with a valuation $u_h\in[u_m-w,u_m)$ keep a home the market values more, and each such home loses $\ell=u_m-u_h$ a year, somewhere between zero and $w$. This is the misallocation the old exclusion created; a reform that ends the exclusion removes it. If valuations are spread evenly across that interval, the average loss is the Harberger triangle
\begin{equation}
 E[\ell\mid\text{allocation switcher},w]
   =\frac{1}{w}\int_0^w \ell\,d\ell=\frac{w}{2}.
 \label{eq:triangle}
\end{equation}
We set $\kappa=\WViHarberger$, the value implied by equation~(\ref{eq:triangle}) under uniform valuations, so that the average loss is $\kappa w$. Uniformity is an assumption about a finite reform, not something the deadline rush reveals \citep{chetty2009sufficient}.

The loss in a year is then the number of homes the reform moved, times the share of those that switched to a higher-value user, times $\kappa$, times the wedge the heir loses. We take each piece from the deed evidence. The calibration uses the \WViParcels\ California homes whose first exit in 2018--2019, before the reform reached the ballot, was a deed in the Section~\ref{sec:prop19} measure, about \WViParcelsPerYear\ a year. Their mean wedge, zeros included, is \$\WViWedgeMean\ in 2023 dollars (the January 2017 wedge, converted with the CPI-U), and we aggregate with the mean because totals need means. \WViSharePositive\ percent have a positive wedge. The number of homes comes from the shortfall of Section~\ref{subsec:decline}. It is measured against the monthly counterfactual of Section~\ref{subsec:design}, California's count in the same month of 2019 scaled by the change in the other states, with the counterfactual also scaled by California's own change in market sales, so that it nets out market sales as the triple difference does. Against it, California's Section~\ref{sec:prop19} measure falls \WViShortfallDeeds\ deeds short over the \WViHoleMonths\ months from March 2021 to June 2024, or \WViShortfallDeedsYear\ a year, which at \WViDeedsPerParcel\ deeds per parcel is \WViShortfallParcelsYear\ parcels a year. Without the market-sale adjustment the shortfall is \WViShortfallDeedsCF\ deeds; the grid reports it as a sensitivity. The share of missing parcels that were reallocated, $\rho$, is not identified: a missing deed may be a postponed succession, a title held in another vehicle, or a family that kept the home for reasons the tax never touched. Our central case sets $\rho=\WViAlpha$.

An heir who moves in is protected. As Section~\ref{subsec:framework_v2} shows, an heir who makes the home a principal residence keeps the old assessment up to the value cap, so such an heir loses the wedge only above the \$\WViValueCap\ allowance, which is \WViShareAboveCap\ percent of the wedge. We treat the heir as an occupant when the buyer's mailing address matches the property, which holds for \WViOccupancyShare\ percent of exits and is an upper bound on occupancy. Occupants therefore lose little, and the rest lose all of it.

The central case is \$\WViLossCentral\ million a year, with $\rho=\WViAlpha$, the shortfall net of market sales, the occupancy proxy and $\kappa=\WViHarberger$. The range runs from \$\WViLossLow\ million, when only the homes beyond the path of California's spouse deeds count (the spouse comparison of Section~\ref{subsec:decline}) and $\rho=\WViAlpha$, to \$\WViLossHigh\ million, when every parcel in the central shortfall is reallocated ($\rho=\WViAlphaHigh$). Appendix Table~\ref{tab:welfare_grid} reports the whole grid of occupancy assumptions, missing-parcel counts and reallocation shares. These rows are scenarios, not confidence intervals.

The loss rests entirely on attributing the hedged decline to the reform. If the fall in family deeds after 2021 reflects the rise in rates, refinancing, or a California-wide shift in how homes pass between people, as the caveats of Section~\ref{subsec:decline} allow, then the number of homes the reform moved is zero and so is the loss. If one accepts the attribution, the misallocation that the old exclusion created and the reform removes is about \$\WViLossCentral\ million a year in a state with \WedgeParcels\ single-family and condominium parcels on the roll. It is a gain from the reform, not a cost of it. Three things hold the figure down: the wedge is modest for most homes, heirs who move in lose only the part above the cap, and only some missing transfers are homes that changed hands. The larger numbers on the grid need every heir to be a non-occupant, every missing deed to be a reallocated home, or the shortfall without the market-sale adjustment. Our data cannot show any of these. The figure is also a flow for one year of missing transfers. A tipped heir loses $\ell$ in every year the home is held, so the present value of the misallocation exceeds the annual figure by an amount that depends on how long heirs hold the homes, which we do not estimate.
