\section{Data and the Anatomy of a Non-Market Transfer}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Deeds}

Property records come from CoreLogic's Owner Transfer file
\citep{corelogic_data}, obtained through an academic license, which compiles
the universe of recorded deeds from county recorder offices. A deed is filed
whenever the legal ownership of a parcel changes---not only when a house is
sold. The same administrative trail that records a \$400,000 sale records a
mother quitclaiming her house to her son for nothing. Market-based datasets
(MLS listings, repeat-sales indices, mortgage originations) discard these
zero-price events by construction. Here they are the object of study.

The extract covers all fifty states and the District of Columbia. Coverage
is dense from 2007 through mid-2024; we use 2007--2023 for annual statistics
and treat 2024 as a partial year. Restricting to residential parcels and
deduplicating to one event per parcel per derived sale date yields 148.7 million
deed events (144.0 million in 2007--2023), linked through a permanent parcel
identifier that follows each house across successive owners.

\subsection{Classifying transfers}

CoreLogic supplies an arm's-length category and an indicator of a potential relationship between the parties. We combine these fields with party names to construct mutually exclusive operational classes. The indicator does not independently verify kinship, and the available extract-specific documentation does not establish its generation algorithm. In particular, flagged deeds can involve companies, trusts, retained owners, or incomplete names. Our main family-transfer proxy combines the same-surname, estate/executor, and other interfamily classes below; trusts and same-person retitles remain separate.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Market sale}: arm's-length category, not flagged
        interfamily, with no price floor because non-disclosure states
        withhold recorded prices on many genuine sales (78.5 million events,
        2007--2023, of which 8.7 percent record a zero or missing price).
  \item \textbf{Same-person retitle}: the named principal is the same person
        on both sides of the deed---an owner adding or removing a co-owner,
        potentially at marriage, divorce, or refinancing (7.1 million). The
        matched name remains, but ownership shares can change. These deeds
        are tracked separately and excluded from the family definition.
  \item \textbf{Family transfer, same surname}: flagged interfamily, buyer
        and seller sharing a surname but not the same person, no trust
        detected (13.6 million). This class and a separately coded estate/executor residual
        (1,905 deeds) form the narrower component of our main proxy.
  \item \textbf{Interfamily, no surname match}: flagged interfamily
        without a detected surname match after the earlier exclusions
        (6.1 million). Missing surname fields and transfers involving
        entities can enter this group. This residual enters the main family proxy. It captures possible
        family conveyances missed by a surname match, but a flagged relationship
        need not be kinship or an ownership replacement.
  \item \textbf{Interfamily trust deed}: the interfamily flag is set and a
        primary party name contains a trust keyword (12.1 million). We split
        these by name. A trust that shares a surname with the other party
        but names a different individual marks a trust-mediated family
        candidate (1.19 million under the stricter name rule); adding trust-to-person deeds
        whose trust name carries no given name gives an expanded count of 1.61
        million. The other 10.5 million
        are a person moving property into or out of the person's own trust,
        or trust deeds with no surname link. Neither group enters the main
        family definition; Table~\ref{tab:volumes} reports the trust-mediated
        transfers as a separate range. The split rests on names alone and
        does not verify control.
  \item \textbf{Other non-arm's-length}: the remainder---divorce divisions
        across surnames, title corrections, nominal-price conveyances, and
        foreclosure-related instruments (26.6 million).
\end{enumerate}

The main definition is deliberately broader than the surname-linked component, but neither measure is a verified inheritance count or a formal lower bound on family transfers. A surname match can be coincidental, while spouses and retained ownership interests may be economically different from parent-to-child successions. Other interfamily deeds can capture heirs with different surnames and transactions involving entities or incomplete names. We therefore show this component separately in Figure~\ref{fig:volumes} and Table~\ref{tab:volumes}. Trust distributions can carry genuine inheritances but remain outside the main proxy. These measurement limits matter for interpreting both California's concentration and the response to Proposition~19.

Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} reports observable differences across the classes.
Same-surname family deeds differ sharply from the defined market-sales class: 90 percent of same-surname transfers record a zero or missing
price, against 9 percent of market sales (mostly unpriced sales in
non-disclosure states) and 57 percent of the residual non-arm's-length class.
A missing price alone therefore does not separate a family transfer from a
sale. Forty percent of same-surname transfers travel by quitclaim deed---the
instrument of gift---against 3 percent of market sales. Where a price is
recorded at all in a same-surname transfer, the median is \$97,000, well
under half the \$221,000 median market price:
the comparison is descriptive because the houses and transferred interests can differ. Corporate buyers, who take 12
percent of market purchases, are essentially absent from same-surname
transfers. These patterns are consistent with gifts and succession, but do not verify their prevalence or exclude other transactions. One
note on probate: formal estate keywords (``estate of,'' executor,
administrator) rarely appear in the recorded name fields, and the earlier trust, same-person, and surname rules also take precedence over the estate rule. The small residual estate class (1,905 interfamily deeds) therefore cannot measure all successions; another 56,056 estate-keyword deeds lack the interfamily flag and stay out of the family definition.
