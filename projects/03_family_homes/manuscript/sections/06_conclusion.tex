\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Recorded residential deeds reveal a substantial channel beyond market sales. Our main family-transfer proxy combines same-surname interfamily, estate/executor, and other interfamily conveyances. It contains \DeedFamilyMillion\ million events in 2007--2023, \DeedNationalShare\ per 100 deed transfers nationally. California leads at \DeedCAShare\ per 100, compared with \DeedUTShare\ in Utah. The same-surname-plus-estate component and the residual interfamily component are reported separately because the vendor flag does not independently verify kinship or inheritance. These shares describe recorded deeds, not changes in occupants or distinct homes.

Many properties remain unsold long after such a deed: \FamilyRetainedTen\ percent of main-definition family events in 2008--2018 are not followed by a market sale within ten years. Differences across components and property types caution against interpreting every family conveyance as long-term retention. Attachment, rental provision, title constraints, recording practices, and tax incentives can all contribute.

California's Proposition~19 provides policy variation in the tax advantage of retaining an inherited assessment. In the maintained June 2020--February 2021 window, family deeds exceed the transparent counterfactual by \PolicyAnticPercent\ percent, approximately \PolicyAnticDeeds\ deeds. In 2022--2023, the triple difference implies \WelfareNetPercent\ percent fewer family deeds relative to market sales, approximately \WelfareFlow\ annually. Conventional state-clustered uncertainty is difficult to assess with one treated state. Retiming, earlier information, pandemic shocks, the portability provisions, and substitution into trusts limit the interpretation of the decline as an allocation response.

Our framework separates efficient family retention from retention induced by a tax advantage. Homes with a qualifying family-proxy exit in 2018--2019 carried a median annual wedge of \$\WedgeInheritedMedian\ in 2023 dollars, including zeros, and approximately \$\WedgeInheritedFlowM\ million of potential annual tax savings per pre-reform year's parcel cohort. Those savings are transfers rather than resource losses. If every missing deed instead reallocates a home to a higher-value user, assigning the inherited-home median wedge and a distortion equal to half that wedge yields \$\WelfareDWLCohortMedian\ million in annual gains per cohort; using the mean wedge yields \$\WelfareDWLCohort\ million. These are conditional illustrations. The reallocation share is unidentified, and missing deeds can reflect postponed succession or changes in legal vehicles without a change in housing use.

Longer follow-up, transaction-time exclusion claims and assessments, verified relationships, and occupancy histories would distinguish reallocations from timing and relabeling. The policy question is how often inherited tax advantages change housing use, beyond their effect on recorded conveyances. Our evidence measures the deed channel and its response; the magnitude of realized allocation gains remains open.
