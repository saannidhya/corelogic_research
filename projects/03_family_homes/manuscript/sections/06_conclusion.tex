\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A large part of American housing turnover never touches a market. From 2007
through 2023, we document that county recorders logged 13.6 million family-transfer deeds,
17 for every 100 market sales. This is a conservative count: it requires a
shared surname, so it misses many relatives with different surnames.

The ratio ranged between 0.14 and 0.22 across years, near
0.20 to 0.22 through 2012 and falling to 0.14 by 2022--2023. Much of that
fall is definitional: the share of interfamily deeds without a surname
match, which our count excludes, rose from 17 to 38 percent. We show that this channel is not a recession artifact as it persists through the 2008--2009 recession and beyond, and it is
not evenly spread across states. California records 36 family transfers per 100 market
sales, about twice the national ratio, and it accounts for 39 percent of
the nation's interfamily deeds into or out of trusts.
We hypothesize that the tax advantage of inheriting a below-market property-tax assessment along with the house is a key driver of this concentration. We also show that family transfers are more common for middle-aged homes than for new ones, and less common for the most expensive homes. Once a home passes between
relatives, it tends to stay put. Family homes sell slightly faster than
market purchases in the first two years, then return to the market more
slowly, and three in five homes transferred within families in
2008--2018 had not sold on the open market a decade later. Transaction data
built from prices miss this channel entirely.

California's Proposition~19 provides a natural experiment to test whether the channel responds to tax incentives. 
From June 2020 through the February 2021 deadline, California family deeds
ran 21.4 percent above counterfactual, about 25,100 extra transfers, and
the deadline month shows the largest seasonally adjusted spike in the
series. The 21.4 percent overstates anticipation. California's 2018--2019
base was unusually low, and family deeds were already above counterfactual
in early 2020, before the measure reached the ballot. After
the deadline, the channel shrank. Net of California's own market-sale slump,
a triple difference shows family transfers \WelfareNetPercent\ percent
lower in 2022--2023 than in 2017--2019, roughly \WelfareFlow\ fewer deeds a
year. A synthetic control and a monthly event study trace the same persistent
gap. The early excess deeds equal about 72 percent of the 2021--2022
shortfall, so retiming may explain the first post-deadline years; the
2022--2023 decline is the robust result. Because our count includes spouses,
it is broader than the parent-child transfers Proposition~19 targets.

We develop a framework that separates efficient family retention from retention bought by
a tax advantage. An heir keeps a home when its value to the family exceeds
the market's valuation net of the inherited tax saving, so the saving tips
only those heirs whose margin is smaller than the wedge. We measure that
wedge parcel by parcel. Homes passed down in California in 2018--2019 carried
a median wedge of \$\WedgeInheritedMedian\ a year in 2023 dollars, including
homes with zero wedges. Their total tax savings were about
\$\WedgeInheritedFlowM\ million a year. That sum is a transfer from
other taxpayers to heirs, not a loss to the economy. The deadweight loss is
the smaller triangle under it. If every missing deed were a home now going to
a higher-value user, assigning each home the median wedge and a distortion
equal to half that wedge would yield about \$\WelfareDWLCohortMedian\ million
a year of avoided deadweight loss per annual cohort. This illustration
assumes every missing deed reallocates a home. A missing deed may be a postponed succession, a
title held through a trust, or a change in legal ownership with no change in
who lives in the house.

Three kinds of data would sharpen the answer: longer follow-up on what
happens to homes that did not change hands within families, transaction-time
records of exclusion claims and assessments, and occupancy and rental status
for inherited homes. Together they would show which missing deeds are
reallocations and which are delays or relabelings. The policy question is
not whether families should keep homes. Many heirs value the family house
more than any buyer would, and the tax code should leave that choice alone.
The question is whether inheritance rules in property taxation pay families a premium
to keep homes that someone else would value more, and how often that margin
binds. Proposition~19 suggests the margin binds often enough to change tens of
thousands of recorded family transfers a year in California; how many of those homes would otherwise go to other families remains open. Nevertheless, more evidence is needed to assess how the property tax lock-in prevents the efficient allocation of scarce housing in the United States.
