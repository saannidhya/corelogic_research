\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Recorded deeds reveal a large channel of housing turnover beyond market sales. Our main family measure, deeds between two distinct people, contains \DeedFamilyMillion\ million events in 2007--2023, \DeedNationalShare\ per 100 residential deed events nationally. California leads at \DeedCAShare\ per 100, ahead of \SIIIRunnerUpState\ at \SIIIRunnerUpShare. Adding back the deeds in which the same person or an entity is on both sides gives an upper bound of \DeedInclNationalShare\ nationally. These shares describe recorded deeds, not changes in occupants or distinct homes, and the vendor flag does not verify kinship or inheritance. Many of these deeds are not followed by a sale: \FamilyRetainedTen\ percent of main-family-measure events in 2008--2018 are not followed by a market sale within ten years, although family deeds resell sooner than market purchases in the first two years.

California's Proposition~19 gave families a deadline, and the deadline is the paper's main result. In January and February 2021 the Section~\ref{sec:prop19} measure ran \SIVMainJanFeb\ times a counterfactual built from other states and 2019, and deeds in which a trust passed a home to an heir ran \SIVHeirJanFeb\ times it. The pace fell to \SIVMainDeadlineSign\ and \SIVHeirDeadlineSign\ of its earlier level after February 16, while spouse deeds, which the law does not touch, did not break. The rushed deeds were resold less, as parents who plan to keep a home would expect, but California's spouse deeds changed about as much, so the comparison does not isolate the reform. The decline after the reform is weaker evidence. The triple difference is $\SIVMainDDD$ (state-clustered standard error \SIVMainDDDSE, a measure of scale with one treated state), which fits the reform. But deeds the law does not touch fall as much or more, the fall begins with the rise in rates in 2022, and against California's spouse deeds it is about zero. Against a counterfactual built from 2019 and other states the deficit is about four times the rush, more than retiming would produce, but series the reform cannot deter fall short of that counterfactual by similar amounts. Trust-to-heir deeds rush hardest at the deadline, which suggests that many are lifetime transfers out of a living parent's trust, although the data cannot show whether the parent was alive; net of market sales they do not fall afterwards. With one treated state, inference is limited.

The welfare calculation inherits that weakness. If the decline is the reform, a share \WViAlpha\ of the missing transfers sent a home to a higher-value user, each such switch lost half the wedge on average ($\kappa=\WViHarberger$), and heirs whose mailing address matches the property are treated as occupants, then the misallocation that the old exclusion created and the reform removes is about \$\WViLossCentral\ million a year, within a scenario range of \$\WViLossLow\ to \$\WViLossHigh\ million. If the decline is not the reform, it is zero. The figure stays low because the wedge is modest for most homes, because heirs who move in lose only the part above the value cap, and because only some missing deeds are homes that changed hands. It is a conditional range for one year of missing transfers, not a measured effect of the reform.

Verified relationships, exclusion claims at the time of transfer, and occupancy histories would separate a home that changed hands from a deed that changed names. A state that wants to know what its inheritance rules do can make that possible. Assessors and the Board of Equalization could record the transfer date, the exclusion claimed and the heir's occupancy with the deed. Until then, we have evidence that families moved transfers forward when the tax code set a deadline, and we do not yet know how many homes the code moved.
