\section{Tax Incentives \& Family Transfers: Proposition 19}\label{sec:prop19}

\subsection{Institutional background}

Proposition~13 (1978) fixed each California parcel's assessed value at its
acquisition price, with growth capped at two percent per year; reassessment
to market value occurs only at a change in ownership. Proposition~58 (1986)
exempted parent-to-child transfers from this reassessment---for a principal
residence of unlimited value plus up to \$1 million of assessed value of
other property---and Proposition~193 (1996) extended the exemption to
grandparent-grandchild transfers. A child could therefore inherit not just a
house but its decades-old tax bill, and keep that tax bill while renting the
house out. The Legislative Analyst's Office estimated that 60,000 to 80,000
inherited properties escaped reassessment each year under this regime
\citep{lao2017inheritance}.

Proposition~19, passed by voters on November 3, 2020, narrowed the exclusion
sharply, effective February 16, 2021: it now applies only when the home was
the parent's principal residence \emph{and} the child makes it their own
principal residence within a year, and only up to the inherited taxable value
plus \$1 million (inflation-adjusted); the exclusion for rentals, vacation
homes, and other non-primary-residence property was eliminated, with a
carve-out for family farms \citep{boe2025prop19, lao2020prop19}. For an otherwise eligible non-occupant heir, the reform removes the inherited-assessment advantage. This raises the tax cost of retention relative to sale, although the family may respond by changing paperwork, timing, occupancy, or ownership rather than by listing the home.
County assessors reported a flood of parent-child filings ahead of the
deadline.\footnote{Sonoma County, for example, received roughly five times
its typical volume of parent-child exclusion filings in the weeks before the
February 15, 2021 deadline (North Bay Business Journal, February 2021); the
Los Angeles County assessor accumulated a multi-year backlog of roughly
20,000 claims (Los Angeles County Auditor-Controller, 2025).} The reform's
other arm---expanded portability of assessments for movers aged 55 and over,
effective April 1, 2021---affects market sales rather than family transfers;
market sales therefore need not be an untreated comparison group. The triple difference below uses them as the comparison deed group; the synthetic control does not use them.

Throughout this section, ``family transfers'' means the broad class
(same-surname, cross-surname, and estate/executor deeds), measured monthly by
state from the event panel; results including trust self-transfers are
reported as robustness.

California tracks the counterfactual closely until the June 2020 ballot
placement, then pulls away. From June 2020 through February 2021, actual
family transfers total 267,107 against a counterfactual of 227,576---an
excess of 39,531 deeds, 17.4 percent above counterfactual over the whole
anticipation period. February 2021, ordinarily the
year's quietest month, became the busiest family-transfer month in the
2018--2023 California series. This is the anticipatory bunching that notch
designs recover for taxed market transactions \citep{bestkleven2018notches,
slemrodwebershan2017dc}, reproduced in transfers with no price: families
re-timed successions to bequeath a tax basis. The concentration around the deadline supports a tax-timing interpretation, although monthly counts do not identify the exact legal transfer date or rule out every contemporaneous influence.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}

We compare California with the other 49 states and the District of Columbia.
Identification requires that, absent the reform, California's family-transfer
trajectory would have followed the relevant control trajectory.

\subsubsection{Synthetic control}

Following \citet{abadie2010synthetic}, we construct a convex combination of
donor states using monthly family-transfer counts normalized by each state's
2018--2019 monthly mean. We choose weights to match the January 2018--May
2020 trajectory, ending the fitting window before the June 2020 ballot
announcement. The post-reform effect is the average proportional gap
relative to synthetic California in 2022--2023. Inference uses in-space
placebos ranked by the post/pre mean squared prediction error ratio and
intervals based on donor-state gaps.

\subsubsection{Triple difference}

We stack family transfers and market sales and estimate
the following triple-difference specification using annual data:
\begin{equation}
  \log N_{gsy}=\alpha_{gs}+\lambda_{gy}+
  \gamma\,\mathbf{1}\{s=\mathrm{CA}\}\mathbf{1}\{y\geq 2022\}
  +\delta\,\mathbf{1}\{s=\mathrm{CA}\}\mathbf{1}\{y\geq 2022\}\mathbf{1}\{g=F\}
  +\varepsilon_{gsy},
\end{equation}
where $N_{gsy}$ counts deeds of group $g\in\{F,M\}$ (family transfers or
market sales) in state $s$ and year $y$, with state and year fixed effects that vary by group. The coefficient $\gamma$ is California's post-reform change in market
sales, and $\delta$ is the triple difference: the change in California family
transfers net of the change in California market sales, each relative to
other states. Netting market sales matters because California's market sales
also fell after 2021, and the reform's portability provisions may affect
them. With group-specific effects, $\delta$ equals the difference between
separate family and market difference-in-differences coefficients. The sample
includes 2017--2019 and 2022--2023, excluding the anticipation and transition
years 2020--2021. Inference reassigns treatment across states and ranks
California's absolute coefficient in the resulting placebo distribution.

\subsubsection{Event-study design}

To separate anticipation from the post-deadline response, we estimate a
monthly event study over 2018--2023 using the following specification:
\begin{equation}
  \log F_{st}=\alpha_s+\lambda_t+
  \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{B}}
  \beta_\tau\,\mathbf{1}\{s=\mathrm{CA}\}\mathbf{1}\{t=\tau\}
  +\varepsilon_{st},
\end{equation}
where $t$ indexes calendar months, $\mathcal{T}$ is the sample period, and
$\mathcal{B}$ pools January 2018--December 2019 as the omitted baseline.
State and calendar-month fixed effects absorb persistent state differences
and common shocks; $\beta_\tau$ measures California's differential change
relative to the baseline.

\subsection{Results: The Rush and the post-reform decline}

Retiming alone would imply a transitory post-deadline trough followed by
recovery. Instead, the gap persists and widens: after the deadline,
California family transfers fall below the counterfactual and stay there
through 2023, while national volumes returned to trend. A donor-weighted synthetic control sharpens the comparison. Following
\citet{abadie2010synthetic}, we build a synthetic California from a convex
combination of the other 49 states and the District of Columbia, with
weights chosen to match California's pre-reform family-transfer trajectory.
Because California is the largest state---an outlier in the \emph{level} of
transfers, which forces a level-matched synthetic onto a single large donor---we
match on each state's volume normalized to its own 2018--2019 mean. The
synthetic reproduces California's pre-reform path to a root mean squared error
of 4.3 percent over January 2018--May 2020 (Figure~\ref{fig:scm}); after the
February 2021 deadline, California's family transfers fall below synthetic
California and remain below it through 2022--2023.

To distinguish the pre-deadline rush from the subsequent decline, we next turn to monthly event-study evidence.
Figure~\ref{fig:event_study} shows monthly event-study results. We regress $\log$ family transfers on California-by-month indicators
with state and year-month fixed effects, the other 49 states and DC as
controls, and the 2018--2019 months as the pooled baseline group.
 As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:event_study}, California sits near its
2018--2019 baseline in early 2020, rises through the anticipation period
as Proposition~19 reaches the ballot and passes, peaks at the February 2021
deadline, and then settles into a persistent decline through 2022--2023\footnote{The
event study is not net of market sales, the triple difference above provides
that comparison.}. Through this event study, we see a clear pattern of anticipation and post-reform decline. Once the proposition is put on the ballot, the salience of the reform rises, and families rush to transfer properties before the new rules take effect. After the deadline, as the reform is implemented, the number of family transfers drops below the baseline and remains low relative to the baseline, indicating a persistent effect of the reform on family transfers.

Although these results are informative and present us with a clear picture of the effect of Proposition 19 on family transfers, we note that the inference is limited to the treated state of California. We have only one treated state, and within that state, all family transfers are affected by the reform. Therefore, these estimates do not have a causal interpretation, as for that, we would need to consider a scenario where we randomize the assignment of the proposition 19 across inherited properties in California. This randomization would allow us to identify the causal effect of the inherited-assessments on family transfers. However, we do not have such a random assignment, and therefore, to better understand the effects of Proposition 19, we need to consider other sources of variation. Next, we develop a simple framework to understand the mechanisms through which Proposition 19 affects family transfers and the potential welfare implications of the reform.
