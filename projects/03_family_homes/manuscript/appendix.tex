\section{Data Construction Details}\label{app:data}

\paragraph{Source and scope.} The CoreLogic Owner Transfer file compiles
recorded deeds from county recorder offices in all fifty states and the
District of Columbia. Our extract contains all record types---deeds of sale,
quitclaims, intra-family conveyances, trust transfers, and foreclosure
instruments. Coverage density is stable from 2007 onward; recording lags
truncate the file around August 2024. All annual statistics use 2007--2023;
the Proposition 19 cohort analyses use outcome windows that close by June 30,
2024.

\paragraph{Deduplication.} Multi-parcel transactions and re-recorded
corrections generate multiple rows per economic event. We keep one record per
parcel-date pair, preferring arm's-length category records and, within
category, the highest recorded consideration.

\paragraph{Classification.} The taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:data} is
implemented with the recorder-derived interfamily indicator, the
arm's-length category code, recorded consideration, and
string features of the buyer and seller name fields (surname equality;
exact same-person matches on either buyer slot, which define the
same-person retitle class; trust keywords; estate, executor, probate, and
administrator keywords).
Missing name fields can move a potentially related-party deed from the surname-linked component into other interfamily, which now enters the main family proxy. Missing names and imperfect full-name matching can also obscure retained owners or entity involvement. The broader proxy therefore improves coverage of possible relatives with different surnames without validating every flagged relationship. Trust deeds and same-person retitles remain separate. National count tables retain a small residual of territory or military geographic codes in the derived panel; state-ranking figures and their U.S. reference line restrict to fifty states and DC. This distinction has negligible effect on the displayed rounded national share but matters for exact count reconciliation.

\paragraph{Absentee recipients.} A recipient is absentee when the buyer
mailing ZIP on the deed differs from the property's situs ZIP, among records
where both are observed (77 percent of events in 2007, rising to 98 percent
by 2023). Time effects absorb common coverage changes; differential changes across states or transfer classes remain a measurement concern.

\subsection{Recorded property types}\label{app:propertytypes}

We retain the Owner Transfer land-use and property-indicator codes as
text. Detailed labels use five entries from a
\href{https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1606747}{published CoreLogic crosswalk};
remaining codes are retained in the data without an asserted detailed
label. The property indicator provides a broader cross-check: in this
extract, indicator 11 accompanies both condominium and apartment land-use
codes. It therefore cannot distinguish those assets by itself.

Types are static recorded characteristics and may differ across records
for a parcel. Counts are parcel-date deeds, not dwelling units. In the
full source audit, only 13 percent of apartment-coded rows carry the
residential flag required by the existing panel. Apartment comparisons
therefore do not describe the complete multifamily sector. We preserve
the original residential restriction for this reproduction.

Type-specific five-year resale comparisons vary. Among 2008--2018 cohorts, weighting family and market events to the same market state/cohort distribution gives sale rates of \TypeResaleFamilyCondo\ versus \TypeResaleMarketCondo\ percent for condominiums, \TypeResaleFamilyDuplex\ versus \TypeResaleMarketDuplex\ percent for duplexes, \TypeResaleFamilySF\ versus \TypeResaleMarketSF\ percent for single-family properties, and \TypeResaleFamilyTownhouse\ versus \TypeResaleMarketTownhouse\ percent for townhouses. These comparisons use the wider main family proxy. They do not identify an effect of inheritance or distinguish retention from settlement and recording practices.

The same-surname comparison at ten years also differs by recorded type.
Kaplan--Meier estimates leave 66.7 percent of duplex family properties
unsold versus 58.8 percent of market purchases, and 59.9 versus 55.9
percent for single-family properties. For condominiums, the ordering
reverses: 47.7 percent of same-surname family properties remain unsold
versus 50.4 percent of market purchases. These pooled comparisons do not
hold state and cohort composition fixed.

For November 2020--February 2021, the 2019-scaled counterfactual gives excesses of \TypeAnticSF\ percent for single-family properties, \TypeAnticCondo\ percent for condominiums, and \TypeAnticDuplex\ percent for duplexes. These are descriptive comparisons without type-interaction inference or direct measures of statutory eligibility. Building type alone does not establish whether a property is a qualifying principal residence.

\section{Robustness Checks}\label{app:robustness}

\paragraph{Trust-inclusive triple difference.} Adding all interfamily trust deeds to the main family proxy produces a volume triple difference of \TrustAllLog\ log points. Adding only trust-to-person distributions produces \TrustDistributionLog\ log points. The main family definition continues to exclude trusts. The all-trust category includes substantial transfers into trusts and other title activity, so this comparison changes the economic content and weighting of the deed aggregate. Distributions are closer to a succession channel, but direction and name fields do not independently verify an inheritance or a change in beneficial ownership. These sensitivities cannot rule out substitution into legal vehicles rather than a change in housing use.
