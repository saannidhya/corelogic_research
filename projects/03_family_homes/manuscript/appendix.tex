\section{Data Construction Details}\label{app:data}

\paragraph{Source and scope.} The CoreLogic Owner Transfer file compiles
recorded deeds from county recorder offices in all fifty states and the
District of Columbia. Our extract contains all record types---deeds of sale,
quitclaims, intra-family conveyances, trust transfers, and foreclosure
instruments. Coverage density is stable from 2007 onward; recording lags
truncate the file around August 2024. All annual statistics use 2007--2023;
the Proposition 19 cohort analyses use outcome windows that close by June 30,
2024.

\paragraph{Deduplication.} Multi-parcel transactions and re-recorded
corrections generate multiple rows per economic event. We keep one record per
parcel-date pair, preferring arm's-length category records and, within
category, the highest recorded consideration.

\paragraph{Classification.} The taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:data} is
implemented with the recorder-derived interfamily indicator, the
arm's-length category code, recorded consideration, and
string features of the buyer and seller name fields (surname equality;
exact same-person matches on either buyer slot, which define the
same-person retitle class; trust keywords; estate, executor, probate, and
administrator keywords).
Name fields are populated for the large majority of records (the seller
surname is non-missing for 84 percent of interfamily events and the buyer
surname for 66 percent); missing names can only push true family transfers
into the ``other interfamily'' class, never into the market class, but imperfect full-name matching can also misclassify retitling. The same-surname series is a narrower proxy, not a formally established lower bound on true inter-person transfers.

\paragraph{Absentee recipients.} A recipient is absentee when the buyer
mailing ZIP on the deed differs from the property's situs ZIP, among records
where both are observed (77 percent of events in 2007, rising to 98 percent
by 2023). Time effects absorb common coverage changes; differential changes across states or transfer classes remain a measurement concern.

\subsection{Recorded property types}\label{app:propertytypes}

We retain the Owner Transfer land-use and property-indicator codes as
text. Detailed labels use five entries from a
\href{https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1606747}{published CoreLogic crosswalk};
remaining codes are retained in the data without an asserted detailed
label. The property indicator provides a broader cross-check: in this
extract, indicator 11 accompanies both condominium and apartment land-use
codes. It therefore cannot distinguish those assets by itself.

Types are static recorded characteristics and may differ across records
for a parcel. Counts are parcel-date deeds, not dwelling units. In the
full source audit, only 13 percent of apartment-coded rows carry the
residential flag required by the existing panel. Apartment comparisons
therefore do not describe the complete multifamily sector. We preserve
the original residential restriction for this reproduction.

Type-specific five-year resale comparisons also differ. Among 2008--2018
cohorts, weighting broad-family and market events to the same market
state/cohort distribution gives sale rates of 32.1 versus 26.4 percent for
condominiums, 19.6 versus 22.0 percent for duplexes, and 24.0 versus 23.4
percent for single-family properties. These comparisons pool family
classes, unlike the main-text same-surname benchmark, and do not identify
an effect of inheriting an asset. They caution against a uniform
interpretation of family deeds as lock-in.

\input{tables/T8_property_retention.tex}

The same-surname comparison at ten years also differs by recorded type.
Kaplan--Meier estimates leave 68.4 percent of duplex family properties
unsold versus 62.8 percent of market purchases, and 61.8 versus 58.4
percent for single-family properties. For condominiums, the ordering
reverses: 48.9 percent of same-surname family properties remain unsold
versus 51.7 percent of market purchases. These pooled comparisons do not
hold state and cohort composition fixed, but the heterogeneity persists
when the family class is restricted to the same-surname proxy.

An exploratory repetition of the transparent 2019-scaled deadline
counterfactual yields anticipation excesses of 29.8 percent for
single-family properties, 36.9 percent for condominiums, and 50.5 percent
for duplexes. These comparisons have neither type-interaction inference
nor direct measures of statutory eligibility; they are descriptive
heterogeneity checks. Building type alone does not establish whether
the property is a qualifying principal residence.

\section{Robustness Checks}\label{app:robustness}

\paragraph{Trust-inclusive volume DiD.} Re-estimating the Proposition 19
volume difference-in-differences with trust self-transfers included in the
family aggregate yields a CA $\times$ Post coefficient of $-0.241$
(s.e.\ $0.027$), a 21 percent decline, confirming that the steady-state drop
in family transfers is not an artifact of reclassification between direct
family deeds and trust vehicles.

\paragraph{Synthetic-control in-space placebo.} The main-text inference for the
steady-state decline assigns the treatment to each donor state in turn and ranks
California's post/pre mean squared prediction error ratio in the resulting
distribution; California is second of fifty-one (Figure~\ref{fig:scm_mspe},
placebo-rank $p = 0.039$).

\paragraph{SCM inference sensitivity.} The saved Conley--Taber-style calculation gives $p=0.167$ and a normalized-index gap interval of $[-0.626,0.185]$. This interval includes zero and is in index-gap units, not percentage-change units. The population-adjusted calculation gives $p=0.188$ under the same alternative procedure. These results qualify the sharper MSPE-ratio ranks; different statistics and assumptions need not give the same inference.

\paragraph{Permutation and pre-fit-adjusted ranks.} As transparent alternatives
to the synthetic control, each donor state is assigned the treatment in turn,
each design is re-estimated, and California's coefficient is ranked in the
placebo distribution. For the annual
family-volume DiD, California ranks 5th of 51 ($p = 0.098$); for the
placebo-netted estimate, 9th ($p = 0.176$). The monthly counterfactual also
reports ranks after scaling each state's policy-window gap by its own
2018m1--2020m10 RMSPE. Under the 2019 same-month baseline, California ranks
3rd of 51 in raw anticipation, February, and 2022--2023 gaps ($p = 0.059$),
but 1st of 51 after pre-fit scaling in all three windows ($p = 0.020$). Using
a 2018--2019 same-month baseline leaves the pre-fit-adjusted ranks unchanged.
For the sold-within-24-months triple difference, $p = 0.67$; for the
estate/trust-seller market-sale DiD, $p = 0.71$ (levels) and $p = 0.63$ (net
of overall market).
