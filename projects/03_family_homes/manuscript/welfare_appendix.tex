\section{Tax Wedge Construction and Welfare Accounting}\label{app:welfare}

\subsection{Derivation and welfare boundary}

The stationary benchmark fixes market prices, housing-service values, and
tax rates. It assumes quasi-linear preferences, equal social weights on
private households and taxpayers, and no unpriced externalities. If sale
incurs real cost $C_i$, the private comparison is
$(u_{hi}-t_iA_{0i})/r\geq p_i-C_i$. Substituting
$p_i=u_{mi}/(r+t_i)$ gives $g_i\leq w_i$, where
$g_i=u_{mi}-u_{hi}-rC_i$ and $w_i=t_i(p_i-A_{0i})$.
The competitive-price and valuation assumptions are essential: observed
selling prices alone do not identify $u_{mi}-u_{hi}$, and rental income
cannot be added to the same tenant service value a second time.
The finite-horizon totals retain this annual-equivalent cost convention;
they do not separately estimate the timing of upfront moving or transaction
costs. Short-run cash resource gains could differ if those costs arrive
before the housing-service benefits.

With full removal of the advantage, the switching interval is $(0,w_i]$.
Conditional uniformity gives $E[g_i\mid w_i,\text{switcher}]=w_i/2$.
If a positive residual advantage $w_{1i}$ remains, the interval is instead
$(w_{1i},w_{0i}]$, and under uniformity the average gain is
$(w_{0i}+w_{1i})/2$, not half the removed advantage. The main calculation
therefore restricts its interpretation to the fully removed margin; it
does not apply the half-wedge formula to the surviving occupant exclusion.

For heterogeneous gains and durations the exact accounting object is
\[
 G_T=\sum_i g_i I_{iT},
\]
where $I_{iT}$ indicates that property $i$ has switched allocation by $T$
and its counterfactual loss would still persist.
Replacing this sum by $\lambda w N_T$ requires a common mean gain among
survivors or the corresponding independence approximation. Survival of
legal ownership is not identical to persistence of a surplus gap. We use
the former only as an explicitly imperfect proxy. The support envelope
$0\leq g_i\leq w_i$ is conditional on the model and the relevant switching
population. It is not a data-identified welfare bound for the whole reform.

\subsection{Episode construction and timing}

Calendar dates are parsed from the eight-digit derived-sale-date field.
Day 00 would be imputed to day 01; other impossible dates would be excluded
and counted. In the current 148,711,427-event panel, neither case occurs.
Explicit parsing reproduces the existing national hazard outputs. This
exercise uses the derived sale date, not a separately verified legal
succession date or recording date.

For California, an episode begins at the first qualifying family deed
after the preceding market sale, or the first observed qualifying deed if
there is no earlier observed sale. Episodes already beginning in 2007 are
not restarted by a 2008 deed. Starts must fall in 2008--2018. Subsequent
family deeds do not restart the clock. A later market sale ends the episode;
a later family deed can begin a new, non-overlapping episode. Each parcel
can therefore appear in more than one episode but cannot appear twice in
the same outstanding stock. A qualifying family deed is an interfamily deed
between parties with the same surname or an estate deed. Broad-family and
same-surname episodes are constructed separately, but they nearly
coincide: estate deeds add 57 California episodes. Missing pre-2007 history
remains a limitation.

We restrict observed endpoints to June 30, 2024, estimate Kaplan--Meier
survival using exact elapsed days and the associated risk sets, and evaluate
it at annual ages using 365.25 days per year. Tied failures precede censoring
at the same duration. Any collision created by date normalization is
resolved to one parcel-date record, preferring a market sale; the current
California audit finds none. The generated episode audit reports counts,
repeated deeds, and the absence of overlapping intervals.

In the prospective accounting, cohorts enter at the beginning of each year
2022--2031, with $S(0)=1$. Beginning-year stocks approximate property-years
within each year; annual gains are discounted as year-end flows to the
beginning of 2022. A five-year delay gives no gains in 2022--2026, and a
ten-year delay gives no gains within this horizon. The real-dollar base is
2023 throughout; the discount-date origin is a separate choice. We do not
claim that uncounted benefits after 2031 vanish. Nor do we count a
pre-reform, grandfathered stock as a gain caused by the reform.

The factor $\alpha$ incorporates unique-property conversion and changes in
allocation, rather than mechanically dividing missing deeds by the national
deeds-per-parcel ratio. Prospective cohorts are interpreted as distinct new
property episodes; aggregate deed estimates cannot verify that assumption.
The constant annual response and its attribution to the inheritance
provision are maintained scenarios, not identified steady-state properties.

\subsection{Constructing the tax wedge}\label{app:wedge}

\input{tables/TA_welfare_data.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:welfare_data} explains why the wedge must be constructed
rather than read off the data. The California property file contains
assessed and calculated values, but its market-value field is empty and its
roll is concentrated in 2008--2009, a decade before the reform. Every term in
equation~\eqref{eq:wedge_hat} therefore starts from that roll and is brought
forward with outside information. The construction is in
\texttt{04a\_build\_wedge.R}; the tables and figures here are generated by
\texttt{04b} and \texttt{04c}.

\paragraph{Universe.} The universe is the latest 2008--2009 record for each
single-family and condominium parcel in California with a positive assessed
value: \WedgeParcels\ parcels, the same parcels at both lien dates. Homes
built or subdivided after the roll are absent. Of the California family
deeds (same-surname interfamily or estate deeds) recorded in 2017--2024,
\WedgeFamLinkage\ percent fall on a parcel in this universe.

\paragraph{Tax rate.} The rate is the 2008--2009 total tax bill divided by
assessed value, with the county median imputed when the ratio is missing or
not finite, and bounded between 0.8 and 2.0 percent. Two features push it
above the ad valorem rate an heir would face on a higher assessment. Bills
can include fixed charges and direct levies that do not scale with value.
And a 2008--2009 rate need not equal the rate in 2017--2019.

\paragraph{Assessment base.} The base rolls the 2008--2009 assessed value
forward with the State Board of Equalization's annual inflation factors,
each at most two percent. An arm's-length market sale of \$30,000 to
\$20 million on or after the roll's lien date resets the base to the sale
price, which then rolls forward. Family transfers never reset the base, which
matches the old parent-child exclusion but not transfers that were
reassessed, for example between siblings. The roll value may itself be a
temporary Proposition~8 reduction: under the lesser-of rule, a home is
enrolled at the smaller of its factored base and its market value, and a
reduced value recovers faster than two percent once prices rise. A
comparison of 2009 assessments with recent purchase prices finds many such
reductions among recent buyers. For those parcels, the rolled-forward base is
too low and the wedge too high. The source file does not identify the
protected base, so we cannot correct this parcel by parcel.

\paragraph{Market value.} The hedonic model is estimated on 2016--2017
sales, with 20 percent of sales held out at random
(Table~\ref{tab:wedge_avm}). Parcels without usable floor area use a model
without it, and the remaining parcels use county-by-type means. Predictions
are made at the January 2017 price level and moved to January 2019 with an
annual county repeat-sales index. Counties with fewer than 500 repeat-sale
pairs use the statewide index. Two limitations matter. The index is
estimated on sales through 2024, so the 2019 values are not built purely from
pre-reform information. And the holdout consists of homes that sold, which
can differ from the long-held homes that dominate the wedge.

\paragraph{External benchmark.} The Legislative Analyst's Office reported a
\$3,000--\$4,000 annual saving for a long-held Los Angeles home
\citep{lao2017inheritance}. For Los Angeles County parcels with no market
sale since 2007, our January 2017 wedge has a median of \$\WedgeLAOMedian\
and a mean of \$\WedgeLAOMean\ across \WedgeLAOParcels\ parcels. ``Long-held''
in our data means no observed sale since 2007, which can include homes
bought more recently than the LAO's example, so the comparison checks
magnitude rather than validating the estimate. The LAO range, converted to 2023 dollars with an assumed 2017
dollar year (CPI-U 245.120 in 2017 and 304.702 in 2023;
\citealp{bls2023cpi}), remains in the sensitivity analysis. The LAO's separate
count of 60,000--80,000 excluded properties a year reflects administrative
eligibility and does not validate the deed response.

\paragraph{Wedge used in the welfare calculation.} The wedges come from the
\WedgeInheritedN\ inherited homes, the parcels whose first ownership change
after January 2017 was a same-surname interfamily or estate transfer recorded
in 2018--2019, measured at the January 2017 lien date and converted to 2023
dollars. Interfamily deeds without a surname match do not end the spell. The
main-text illustration assigns each missing deed the median inherited-home
wedge, \$\WelfareWedgeMedian\ (\$\WelfareDWLCohortMedian\ million a year).
Because the deadweight loss is a sum over heterogeneous homes, the mean,
\$\WelfareWedge, is the natural alternative (\$\WelfareDWLCohort\ million); it
is slightly below the LAO's low endpoint in 2023 dollars, \$\WelfareWedgeLow.
Neither is the wedge of the marginal heir, which the data do not identify.

\subsection{Sensitivity and statistical uncertainty}

\input{tables/TA_welfare_sensitivity.tex}

The full grid comprises 4,050 combinations: two episode definitions (which
nearly coincide),
five conversion factors, three delays, five wedges, three valuation-gap
fractions, three hazard scales, and three discount rates. The wedge menu, in 2017 dollars converted to 2023 dollars, contains the
measured median and mean wedge of inherited homes, the LAO's \$3,000 and
\$4,000 endpoints, and a \$7,000 stress case. Changing cumulative hazards by a factor $k$ uses $S_k(a)=S(a)^k$.
Discount rates are 0, 3, and 5 percent. The main table varies conversion and
delay; the appendix changes other inputs around an illustrative, explicitly
unpreferred $\alpha=0.5$, five-year-delay case.

These calculations condition on the triple-difference reference flow. They do not
turn uncertainty about that flow into statistical confidence bands for
welfare. Its placebo-rank $p=0.118$ and the SCM specifications' different
inference remain material. A nonpositive true allocation response, title-only
responses, a sufficiently long delay, or no net resource gap can eliminate
the calculated gains. Correlation between wedges and persistence, changing
future tax liabilities, endogenous rents and prices, heterogeneous financing
constraints, and real public-service effects are outside this calculation.
